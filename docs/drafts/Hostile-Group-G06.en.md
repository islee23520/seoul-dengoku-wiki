## Common species and variants

It was a mixed stopover in which waterfowl, waders, and raptors overlapped at the waterfront records deck of the Government of the Republic of Korea at central Seoul Station and the drainage ponds of the Seongha Group on the Gangnam Line. Waterfowl ate aquatic plants and floating matter, waders benthic creatures, and raptors rodents and exhausted small birds. There was no unified command and no aim of attacking the city.

From the broad-feather perch to the pursuit wing, these were wild birds of different functional groups. The pond mirror and the open mirror were the water surface of an intermediate stopover itself, and the three-layer flight was a knot in the air where three heights overlapped on one bridge cross-section. The rooftop gutter was a branch of the same waterfowl, using inland fountains and rooftop gutters in the dry season when the wetland dried.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0014"></a>

### Role and Physiology

A controller: an adult waterbird that covers open water first through its size and wingspan, rather than directing the flock with calls. Its eyes distinguish reflections on the water, the colors of work vests, and drone silhouettes; from engine noise and loudspeakers, it can judge only distance. It knows nothing of electronic signals or the water-level numbers inside the sluice gates, and its ankles sink at muddy edges, preventing it from standing there long.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0016"></a>

### Origin

When the fragmented Han River–Nanji wetlands dried up and only the water in front of Seoul Station’s central records deck remained, the first waterbird to arrive occupied the open surface, placing this body at the center. The overlapping shallows of G06-SC1 are its stage; the water left after HC06 turned on the pumps for visibility fixed its occupation in place.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0018"></a>

### Habitat

By day it spreads its flock across the middle of Seoul Station’s central shallow water; when the level falls, it moves to the Gangnam Line drainage pond. It passes over the bridge walkway but does not stay there, and its talons are unsuited to wire perches.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0020"></a>

### Needs and Economy

Aquatic plants and suspended organic matter are its food; safe open water and a short takeoff distance are what it needs to stay. If HC06 lowers the water level, suspended matter gathers in one strip and strengthens its hold. Holding the water level steady increases its numbers and, with them, the risk of train collisions.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0022"></a>

### Life Cycle and Maintenance

It grows larger only during spring and autumn stopovers; outside the migratory seasons, its wings become lighter and the birds disperse. When its feathers are soaked in turbid water, takeoff is delayed, exposing it to pursuit by raptors. If forcibly dispersed for a season, it occupies the same water earlier at the next stopover.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0024"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

It gives no orders. When the water surface narrows, shorebirds are merely pushed toward the edges and raptors toward the wires. Even if H06 Baksol combines them into one score, the HC06 duty officer records altitude and water zones separately. If this bird leaves, the open water is vacated and shorebirds spread as far as the trackside.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0026"></a>

### Escalating Threat

In stage 1, it covers the middle of the narrowed water, blocking the view from the records deck. In stage 2, a single raptor pursuit sends the dense flock surging across the view of the sluice gates and the train arrival and departure tracks. In stage 3, after the same collision occurs three times, the response must set both zone-specific water levels and slowdown times rather than call for extermination.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0028"></a>

### Combat and Response

Gunfire and nets scatter the flock to fountains and rooftop water catchments, making its next stopover unpredictable. Zone-specific water levels, fishmeal-waste collection, and drone altitude limits are the basics. Success is measured by the duration of clear sightlines, zero collisions, and turbidity—not carcasses.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0030"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

Running the pumps longer to expand the open water increases labor and energy use; drying the water merely to gain visibility brings dust, stench, and rodents down on residents. Creating a new shorebird feeding ground outside the depot leaves this bird’s occupation intact while reducing crowding at the edges, but citizens bear the land and dredging costs.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0032"></a>

### Scenario Connections

The overlapping water of G06-SC1 is the stage for this occupation; the slowdown learning of G06-SC2 and the takeoffs during the migratory-bird week of G06-SC3 follow in a chain from the same body.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0037"></a>

### Role and Physiology

A common ecological type: a medium-sized waterbird that follows bands of floating organic matter rather than holding the middle of the water. Its bill sorts plant fragments from fishmeal scraps, and its eyes notice oil slicks before the color of rubbish. It cannot understand vibrations in wires or radar classifications, and gives up diving when turbidity prevents it from seeing the bottom.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0039"></a>

### Origin

During a season when rain washed fishmeal from market unloading into the edges of Seoul Station’s central water, individuals displaced from the open surface split off and pursued only floating matter. Standing water in the unsuccessfully restored wetland was a feeding ground; the longer collection was delayed, the farther their roaming route extended from the unloading area to the drainage pond.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0041"></a>

### Habitat

Ordinarily it circles in the slow current outside the mudline of Seoul Station’s central water. When the wetland dries, it briefly moves to inland park fountains and rooftop water catchments. It visits the Gangnam Line pond only when floating matter gathers there, and does not fly over the train arrival and departure tracks.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0043"></a>

### Needs and Economy

It feeds more on floating organic matter and discarded fishmeal than on aquatic plants; a band of scent, not a clean water surface, sustains it. If HC06 collects the waste, it does not go to the pond. If the waste is left, it follows it low into the unloading alleys, overlapping with pedestrians.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0045"></a>

### Life Cycle and Maintenance

If an oil slick mats its feathers during a stopover season, taking off becomes difficult and it retreats to fountains. After forced dispersal, it searches for the same smell from farther away. When the floating matter is cut off, its numbers fall the following season and it does not join the flock occupying the open water.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0047"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

There is neither command nor a spacing rule. It follows floating matter when it moves; even when Broadwing Perch covers the surface, it avoids conflict by using only the band at the edge. If H06 turns its roaming tracks into a single hostility score, HC06 changes only the waste-collection time.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0049"></a>

### Escalating Threat

In stage 1, rain washes fishmeal from unloading into a band beneath the records deck. In stage 2, the band extends to the drainage pond and waterbirds skim the edge of the depot tracks. In stage 3, the smell reaches fountains and rooftops, turning urban water catchments into temporary stopovers.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0051"></a>

### Combat and Response

Horns and drones drive it into alley puddles while leaving the band of scent in place. Fishmeal collection and covers at the unloading area break the route. Success is measured by the number of incursions onto depot tracks and by turbidity, not carcasses.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0053"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

Leaving fishmeal at the market to keep the pond clear makes unloading workers and pedestrians bear the smell and low flights. Thorough collection reduces this bird’s numbers, but the flock occupying the open water then crowds more densely onto what remains. Providing both unloading-area covers and temporary water outside the pond reduces collisions, but leaves the cost of cleaning water catchments.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0055"></a>

### Scenario Connections

In G06-SC1, the lowered water level gathers floating matter into one band; during the migratory-bird week of G06-SC3, that band stretches beneath the bridge. The forced dispersal of G06-SC2 merely disrupts its learned scent route, making the next run more irregular.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0060"></a>

### Role and Physiology

A common ecological type: a shorebird that sweeps the shallow muddy edge at regular intervals. Its long bill probes for bottom-dwelling organisms, while its eyes first register the distance to neighboring birds and the colors of work vests. It cannot take off from open water; engine noise tells it only direction, and it cannot read electronic guidance.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0062"></a>

### Origin

When waterbirds covered the remaining water, shorebirds landed on the exposed mudline and this spaced gait became established. The unpartitioned edge of G06-SC1 is the basis of its form; as the mud extended to the trackside inspection passage, its range began to overlap with pedestrian traffic.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0064"></a>

### Habitat

The exposed mudflat beneath Seoul Station’s central records deck and the receding-water edge of the Gangnam Line pond are its main habitats. When the water level is steady, the birds spread farther apart; when it drops sharply, they pack together as far as the inspection-passage ledge. They do not perch on wires or pylons.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0066"></a>

### Needs and Economy

It feeds on bottom-dwelling organisms in shallow water; if the mudflat dries or dredging turns over the bottom, its bill probes in vain. When HC06 holds the water level steady, it raises the density of bottom-dwelling life but also increases track incursions. Unilateral drainage expands the mudflat but exposes food all at once, drawing the birds into a crowd.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0068"></a>

### Life Cycle and Maintenance

When bill tips wear down during the spring stopover, spacing narrows and disputes increase. A flock caught in nets for one season hides deeper in the inspection passage on its next migration at the mere sight of a human silhouette.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0070"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

There is no leader; the birds simply maintain their spacing. When waterbirds cover the surface, they are pushed to the edge. When a raptor looks down from a wire, their spacing collapses and they take off together. If H06 combines shorebirds and waterbirds into one count, the HC06 duty officer records the number of birds at the edge separately.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0072"></a>

### Escalating Threat

In stage 1, they land only on the mudflat beneath the deck. In stage 2, their spacing extends into the trackside inspection passage, overlapping with workers’ feet. In stage 3, a raptor pursuit sends the flock surging up across the height of the train arrival and departure tracks.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0074"></a>

### Combat and Response

Mass netting drives birds that have learned the spacing into alley drains, increasing mistaken captures. It is safer to use zone-specific water levels to keep the mudline outside the tracks and maintain a shallow feeding ground beyond the depot. Success indicators are the number of incursions and the density of bottom-dwelling life.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0076"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

Creating a new shorebird feeding ground outside the depot reduces crowding at the edge, but shifts the land and dredging burden to another district. Drying the mud to drive the birds out kills bottom-dwelling life and moves the next season’s stopover to the bridge walkway. Giving up one strip of walking space to preserve the mudflat sacrifices a little visibility in exchange for clear tracks.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0078"></a>

### Scenario Connections

The zone-specific water levels and outside feeding ground of G06-SC1 are the negotiated solution for this spacing; in G06-SC3, movement along the edge sets off a chain of movements at other heights. Drone dispersal in G06-SC2 breaks the spacing, causing the birds to gather more tightly at the next inspection time.

### Role and Body

An ordinary ecological type, this small waterbird stays in the shade beneath the records deck by day and comes out onto the water only at dusk. Its eyes startle first at fluorescent vests and drone silhouettes, while its nose distinguishes human sweat and disinfectant more readily than decay. Under electric lights at night, it loses its bearings and cannot connect itself to a radar blip.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0085"></a>

### Origin

When the sluice observation boat became busy during the day, individuals avoiding open water began using the shade beneath the deck as a place to nap. Repeated low-noise inspection windows taught them to take off at dusk, and as fluorescent vests became more common, they retreated deeper into the shade.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0087"></a>

### Habitat

Its daytime resting places are the shaded pilings and the space beneath the railings of Seoul Station Central's waterfront records deck; only at dusk does it move to the edge of the Gangnam Line pond. It avoids the bridge walkway and the airspace above the depot tracks at midday, and hides in the shade of rooftop catch basins if the wetland dries out.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0089"></a>

### Needs and Economy

It feeds on insects at the water's surface and thin organic films at dusk; shade and low noise are necessary for it to remain. If the deck stays open all day, it moves into the shade along the depot tracks, increasing collision risk. Restricting daytime access, however, delays recordkeeping work.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0091"></a>

### Life Cycle and Maintenance

If repeatedly startled by fluorescent colors over a season, it begins taking off earlier at dusk, bringing its flights into the peak train period. If its feathers rot in the damp shade, its night flights shorten; after exposure to forced lighting, it hides deeper inside structures at its next stopover.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0093"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

It does not lead a flock, competing only for shaded spots. Even when the broad-winged roost covers the water, their daytime spaces do not overlap. If H06 reports heat spots in the shade as a bird flock, a person distinguishes by sight whether the birds are resting or taking off.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0095"></a>

### Escalating Threat

At stage 1, it stays only in the shade beneath the deck and does not obstruct sightlines. At stage 2, as fluorescent vests become more common, its dusk takeoff veers toward the arrival and departure tracks. At stage 3, its shaded refuge shifts beneath vehicles on the depot tracks, bringing it into the same space as the low-noise inspection window and maintenance workers.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0097"></a>

### Combat and Response

Loudspeakers and flashes drive birds in the shade farther under dark vehicle bodies. Toning down vest colors, changing the lighting beneath the deck to indirect light, and keeping drones out of the shaded flight zone delay takeoff. Success means zero maintenance injuries and clear sightlines, not carcasses.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0099"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

Closing the deck by day to give its shade to the birds delays sluice records; keeping fluorescent colors sends takeoffs toward the tracks. Announcing one hour at dusk as a low-noise window reduces time for recordkeeping work, but means maintenance workers and birds need not share the same shade.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0101"></a>

### Scenario Connections

The low-noise inspection window in G06-SC2 sets this bird's takeoff clock, and the crowd inspecting the sluice in G06-SC3 pushes it from the shade toward the depot tracks. The narrow water surface in G06-SC1 draws it to the edge of open water only at dusk.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0106"></a>

### Role and Body

A specialist waterbird that reads the band of food gathered where the water surface slopes when the pump runs. Its ears distinguish the pump's low rumble from train engines, and its eyes track how fast the water level drops by a handspan. It cannot read the operating schedule itself or know how much water lies beyond a closed sluice.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0108"></a>

### Origin

When HC06 began turning on the pump at the same time to improve visibility, it learned within a season that floating matter and fry gathered on the sloping water surface. This is a mechanical clock learned in one season, not a change in migration timing across generations; changing the schedule breaks the learning.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0110"></a>

### Habitat

It stays on the water within sight of the Seoul Station Central pump outlet and near the water-level gauge at the Gangnam Line pond. It disperses during the day when the pump is idle. On days when the XT01 wind corridor coincides with pump operation, the slope grows steeper and it follows the food as far as beneath the bridge.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0112"></a>

### Needs and Economy

It feeds on organic matter concentrated as the water level falls. Running the pump irregularly breaks its learned pattern and drives the birds toward the pond. Keeping the time fixed stabilizes stopover density but increases the risk of overlap with trains at that hour.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0114"></a>

### Life Cycle and Maintenance

Once it stores the pump's low rumble as a safety signal for a season, it gathers at the same time on its next migration. If its feathers are damaged by the swift current, it cannot follow the slope and settles at the edge of the waders' area. A single forced drainage brings its arrival the following season forward by a day.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0116"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

It does not direct the flock; it only signals the slope. Other waterbirds simply gather along that band. Even if H06 raises the density reading on radar, HC06 reassesses pump operation when the pump logs and visual observations disagree.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G06-heading-0118"></a>

### Escalating Threat
