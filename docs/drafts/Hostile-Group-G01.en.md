## Common species and variants

In the wetlands and waste slopes outside the Sluice in the western Han River, family groups lived scattered, one to each sow. They fed on organic food waste, salt, and shallow root crops, and they learned the sealing hours and the gaps when the sluice vibration stopped in order to approach. The adult females opened each move and the subadults scouted ahead, while the lone males used the same feeding grounds without following those decisions.

An ordinary flood boar does not leave the family-group ecology even when it wraps its back in a mud bath and a salt crust. The iron-plate, fused-plate, and venom-tusk lines are mutants split off by deep sewer contamination, and a low-frequency call binds only that bloodline. The Mud Iron Wall and the Steel Wetland are places where family labor has piled up, not the body of the great boar itself.

**Life in the endless cold.** Wild boar live by the warmth people leave behind. With no growing season, they root under snow at heated waste heaps and dig for the salt of cooling-water pools; a herd follows the plume of a station vent the way it once followed a stream. The old words for the rains and the dry season now name the pulse of meltwater from heated stations and the frozen weeks between them.

**Threat and the cost of fighting.** A herd on a heated slope can break a fence line in one night. This is a low threat by the standards of the stations, and the doctrine is to spend as little as possible on it. Support teams with nets, fences and bait carry most of the work, and missile infantry fire only when a crossing has to be cleared. Recon drone teams are flown on a short leash to read heat on the surface, because every charge drawn from the cells in the cold is hard to replace. War machines are never sent, since the fuel they burn costs more than the animals are worth. The real costs are rounds that miss in the dark, bites that need medicine, and the hours of the people on the fence; lives are seldom lost unless a patrol is cut off in the snow.

## Individuals and battlefield formation

The iron-plate line's absolute command holds only inside the resonance range of the metal and venom variant bloodlines, and does not direct the whole of the sow family groups. With its hooves it reads the sluice's low frequency layered over the smells of scraps and disinfectant, and it cannot decipher a distant heat source or electronic noise. When the central female disappears, the group sniffs the young's scent for about 48 hours and then scatters by family, and the great boar's call does not fill that gap.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0014"></a>

### Role and Body

The alpha controller of the Wild Boar ruled over a heap of refuse in the lower Han River. Its massive body, four meters long, carried metal wreckage on its back and shook the surrounding sewers.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0016"></a>

### Origin

A mutant born in the polluted wetlands swept by the G01-SC1 Great Flood took command of the early group. It later led the entire colony's breeding and territorial expansion, reshaping the southern Han River ecosystem.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0018"></a>

### Habitat

It settled deep in the sewer tunnels connected to the flooded zone south of Seoul Station. Only during the monsoon rains, when the waters rose, did it surface to scout the surroundings.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0020"></a>

### Needs and Economy

It relied on decaying organic matter and rusted metal ions as its main energy sources. By consuming exclusively the resources collected by the entire group, it sustained the colony's survival. G01-SC2 confirmed that when energy ran out, the whole colony entered prolonged hibernation.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0022"></a>

### Life and Maintenance

After a single breeding season in its lifetime, it continually renewed the iron armor on its back. It welded metal fragments brought by its subordinates into place with its own bodily fluids, strengthening its defenses.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0024"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

It issued absolute commands through low-frequency calls. All boars obeyed at once, while E02 through E04 interpreted the signal and relayed it to subordinate individuals. This structure kept the colony's hierarchy stable.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0026"></a>

### Escalating Threat

First it unleashed a territorial warning call to threaten intruders. Then it charged with its massive body, scattering the metal fragments on its back in every direction. Finally, it summoned the entire colony to surround its targets with overwhelming numbers.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0028"></a>

### Combat and Response

If field personnel installed a high-frequency sound generator, it could induce resonance in Ironclad and create cracks. On failure, the Great King's charge would collapse the sewer walls, flood the surrounding area, and sharply raise the cost of evacuating citizens. The success signal was the sound of cracks appearing across Ironclad's surface.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0030"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

It was a being that maintained the colony's ecological balance. Excessive hunting would cripple the entire sewer system and contaminate drinking water for residents of southern Seoul. Limited negotiation was possible in G01-SC3, but refusal led the colony to actively invade human settlements.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0032"></a>

### Scenario Links

It played a key role in G01-SC1, G01-SC2, and G01-SC3.

Its long snout read the surrounding scents while buried in wetland mud. Its thin, flexible legs moved quickly through the current.

The flood-survivor lineage of G01-SC1 grew out of the Great King's first litter. It later formed a scouting network responsible for the colony's outer perimeter.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0041"></a>

It moved between shallow pools on the edge of the flooded zone and underground drains. By day it hid in the mud and was active only at night.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0043"></a>

It fed by catching decaying aquatic plants and small fish. By bringing the freshest portions to the Great King, it circulated both information and resources through the colony.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0045"></a>

It shed its coat every two years, covering its broken snout tip with mud and saliva to form new sensory receptors. In the process, it updated information about the surrounding terrain.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0047"></a>

It sensed the smell of danger before the Great King's low call reached it. Its distinctive snout-call signaled E03 and E04, putting the whole group on alert.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0049"></a>

First it revealed its position with a low snout-call. Then it gathered nearby individuals to encircle targets as a group. Finally, it lured in the Great King to deliver the decisive blow.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0051"></a>

### Combat and Response

If field personnel lured it into a trap set in the mud, its snout caught in the trap and slowed its movement. On failure, the scout summoned the group to block sewer pipes and cut off civilian routes. The success signal was muddy foam flowing from its snout.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0053"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

Leaving fresh organic refuse at a designated spot temporarily pushed its boundary back. But if the entire colony faced food shortages, it expanded its scouting range into human settlements and contaminated water sources.

Its powerful jaws broke rusted metal pipes, and its acidic saliva dissolved corrosion. Rust flakes attached to its torso blended into the surrounding environment.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0062"></a>

An individual raised in a heap of sewage-treatment refuse came to manage the colony's resource cycle. Records from G01-SC2 show that these individuals improved the colony's waste-processing efficiency.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0064"></a>

### Habitat

It roamed mainly along the floors of sewer tunnels piled with metal refuse. It was most active around pools of stagnant acidic water.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0066"></a>

### Needs and Economy

It fed on rusted metal and corroded organic matter. By breaking them down, it supplied reusable ions to the whole colony. When supplies ran short, the colony's scavenging activity stopped.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0068"></a>

### Life and Maintenance

To survive in acidic environments, it periodically renewed its skin. It washed the rust from around its jaws and grew new acid-secreting glands.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0070"></a>

### Behavior and Hierarchy

It cleaned refuse independently, even without the Great King's orders. But when it heard the call, it immediately stopped scavenging and moved into combat formation.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0072"></a>

### Escalating Threat

It sprayed acidic saliva to corrode enemy equipment. Then it narrowed the encirclement with nearby individuals, trapping its targets in place.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0074"></a>

### Combat and Response

If field personnel sprayed neutralizer, the acid was neutralized and the scavenger's attack power fell sharply. On failure, the acid dissolved field equipment and reduced movement speed by 70%. The success signal was white smoke rising as the neutralizer reacted with the saliva.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0076"></a>

### Negotiation and Moral Choice

Leaving a large quantity of metal refuse in a designated tunnel concentrated its scavenging there, clearing the surrounding area for a while. But over-supplying refuse sharply increased the colony's size and led to frequent sewer blockages.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G01-heading-0078"></a>

## Habitat record

After the water of the western Han River split the embankments from the green axis, a night order unlike the one before took hold in the wetlands outside the Sluice.

The wild boar family groups that came down from the Bukhansan and Anyangcheon corridors crossed the severed green belts of Yeouido and Yeongdeungpo and settled at the food-waste collection yards and waste slopes, and from the cooling-waste heaps sealed by HC01 Yeouido Computing House they drew the salt to last the winter.

After floods the wild boar came down into the semi-basement alleys to root through trash bags and shallow root crops.

Urban incursions, shifts in food sources, and severed movement routes could be explained by ordinary wildlife observation, but the behavior of remembering the sealing hours and the sluice operating cycle to time an approach, and the managed relationship with HC01, were an order that arose only in this region after the collapse.

The territory of the Wild Boar was set less by lines drawn on a map than by a continuity of water level and smell.

The core habitat was the wetland and waste slopes outside the sluices of the Sluice, and in the dry season and winter they moved along the cut green belts toward the Government of the Republic of Korea at central Seoul Station and along the axis of night-market leftovers.

When refugee columns pushed down from the XT01 Imjin River Gate Theater, the food traces left on the northwestern roads became new corridors.

When the flood level crossed the warning line, the lowland family groups climbed all at once to the upland stairs and overlapped with human walkways.

This movement was not a march to attack the city but a choice to seek food and dry ground together, yet the semi-basement residents and sluice workers could not easily feel that difference.

The economy they relied on consisted of organic food waste, salt, and shallow root crops.

Soft plastics and coolant pools also served as secondary resources, and the droppings left after feeding nourished the wetland microbial mats and the downstream vegetation, making feeding grounds again.

When HC01 delayed sealing its collection tanks, family groups crowded onto a single spot, and when it tightened the seals too far, the herd turned toward the household alleys.

Unlicensed hunting for meat and hide left immediate food and trade goods, but it scattered the survivors and distorted even the passage-safety records.

Because a measure meant to protect civilian rations could increase food raids in vulnerable alleys, the cost of this ecology always moved to someone else's doorstep.

Before the monsoon, the family groups split up, one to each sow with young, to scout feeding grounds and escape routes.

Right after a flood they briefly joined the upland food axis, but they did not become one great herd.

When an adult female began to move, the subadults scouted ahead, and the lone males used the same feeding grounds without following that decision.

When the central female disappeared, the family group swept the same area over and over for about 48 hours, following the scent of the young and the sows, and then scattered.

Only when the survival rate of the young stayed high for two seasons in a row did a family group split, and when winter salt sources and mud wallows ran short, it chose long-range movement over breeding.

They could learn the fence positions and sealing hours of the moment, but that was not a long-term change in the body.

Nocturnal activity and the avoidance of people could persist over several generations, and heavy trapping backfired by removing the boldest animals first and leaving only the more secretive lines.

Yet the more secretive they became, the longer it took them to find food, and the slower their young grew.

Through their hooves and the ground, the Wild Boar read the opening and closing of the sluices and the low-frequency vibration of vehicle generators, and judged by layering the smell of food over the smell of human disinfectant.

At close range they also responded to warmth and air currents carried on the wind, but they could not precisely tell distant heat sources apart or work out the meaning of electronic noise.

H01 Hannuri only compared and marked the infrared masses that looked like people, generators, or family groups; it was not the final judge.

In heavy wind and rain or thick cooling fog, the thermal images blurred and a lone male and subadults looked like a single cluster, so a human scouting team had to confirm the escape routes and the traces of young first.

When a family group was mistaken for a single hostile group and its central female was removed, two days of unpredictable searching behavior filled the place of the vanished order.

The crisis grew in three stages.

At first only the night hoofprints multiplied at the collection yards and unauthorized plots outside the sluice, and contact with people was rare.

Next the drainage ditches, the sealing tape, and the water-inspection passages were damaged, and the food convoys and sluice maintenance fell behind.

At the last, flood levels and refugee leftovers coincided and the family groups took over the sluice stairs.

Noise barriers and flashes could briefly drive back the scouts in front, but for the main body food detours, relocated salt pools, and route fencing worked better.

Removal led by firearms scattered the family groups into every alley and increased the harm to civilians, so it remained only a last resort.

The success of a response was judged not by the number of carcasses but by the reduction in collection-yard intrusions, the time to reopen passage, and alley damage.

G01-SC1 ‘The First Hoof Outside the Sluice’ began when the prints of a sow and her young appeared at the Sluice collection yard on the day before the monsoon forecast.

The family group remembered the sealing hours and approached in the gaps when the sluice vibration stopped.

The Sluice and HC01 could avoid contact by delaying night operations, but civilian rations were delayed with them.

Opening a wetland detour with scent bait cost labor and resources at once but could create a long-term movement axis.

Trapping only the sow looked fastest, but H01 then recorded, for the next 48 hours, the heat sources of the young returning again and again in search of her.

The judgment in that moment meant putting people's mealtimes and the survival time of a family group with young on the same table.

In G01-SC2 ‘The Price of the Salt Pool,’ HC01 moved up its collection of cooling waste, and the herd then shifted its feeding grounds to the semi-basement alleys toward central Seoul Station.

It came to light that removing the salt source did not make the family groups disappear but pushed them into a more dangerous living sphere.

Leaving a managed pool in the wetland held the danger in one place but meant accepting the cost of continuous watch.

Putting alley food collection first reduced the harm to residents but left other rations and maintenance labor short.

Lowering density through hunting permits gave clear short-term results but strengthened the survivors' learned avoidance of people and their night intrusions.

Whichever choice was made, there was no escaping responsibility for whether to leave a controlled danger in a safe zone or to shift the burden onto a less visible vulnerable zone.

G01-SC3 ‘Passage Rights on the Flood Line’ broke out when the leftovers of the XT01 refugee column and the rising flood level drew people and family groups onto the same sluice stairs.

Splitting the evacuation times reduced direct clashes, but those whose turn came late had to endure the water and the dark longer.

Diverting the leftover convoys into a bait corridor could lead the herd toward the wetland, but it used up HC01's transport resources and sealed containers.

Opening the sluice first to create a brief high water had a chance of hastening the move, but it could flood the downstream workshops in advance.

So the agreement HC01 brokered bound together the food sealing hours, a night passage window, a cultivation buffer strip, and separate collection of the XT01 leftovers.

Extermination was not the goal, and choosing coexistence meant threat reports never fully disappeared.

In exchange, wetland purification, downstream vegetation, and the working routes of the sluice laborers could be kept.

Negotiating with the Wild Boar was less a promise to tame the wild than a contract limiting human desire and convenience so that the two sides' timetables did not overlap.

Long-term management depended on joint patrols in which free-city, HC01, and the semi-basement residents redrew the boundaries of food and passage every season.

Before the monsoon they recorded each sow's prints and mud wallows without tagging, and after a flood they adjusted the night food-disposal hours and the positions of the managed salt pools so that the family groups did not lose their familiar escape routes.

The key indicators were alley intrusions per hundred nights, the time taken to clear the sluice stairs, the proportion of traces with young, the area of rooted-up ground, and the distance of human encounters by corridor, and carcass counts were kept only in the supplementary record.

Even if intrusions fell, a sharp drop in traces of young or shorter encounter distances were read not as stability but as trapping pressure and food shortage.

When a fence collapse or a mistaken capture scattered the family groups, pursuit and night collection stopped for 48 hours, water and salt were spread out in the directions where old droppings and prints remained, and the passage windows were then restored step by step.

Recovery was confirmed less by the return of the central female than by whether the young passed through the same corridor twice in a row and alley re-intrusions decreased.

When this procedure held for a long time, residents too came to put out food waste at set hours, which could reduce the rats' food sources and the damage to underground wiring.

Yet when hoof disturbance concentrated in predictable corridors, the reed belts thinned and the floating trash of the next flood reached the sluice intakes faster, a secondary cost, so even the safe coexistence zones had to be rested every few years and the detour wetlands opened in turn.
