## Raccoon Dogs in the Winter

The raccoon dog belongs to the dog family, but it climbs trees and digs burrows well, and it is a rare species that rests for long stretches in winter in a state close to deep sleep. Since the Great Blackout, river embankments, drainage pipes, and spaces under manholes in a city with fewer people have become good dens for raccoon dogs. As the artificial winter lengthens, their rest shortens, and they come down to station vents and the basements of surface buildings in search of food. There are no mutated individuals and none that commands a fight.

Raccoon dogs live as a pair with their young. The oldest female has priority in carrying the young and in passing first through narrow passages. There is no commanding of a group. By day they rest under manholes and in pipes, and at night they circle within about fifty meters of an exit looking for food.

The danger comes in three forms: raiding food stores and food-waste yards; slipping into underground spaces through vents, shutter gaps, and drains; and rabies, canine distemper, mange (sarcoptic mites), and parasites carried by raccoon dogs that pass to people and livestock. Raccoon dogs do not attack people, but if cornered they bite.

## Ecology

| Item | Record |
|---|---|
| Winter movement | At the start of winter they fatten up in order to sleep, but if food is short they roam without resting. They come out of burrows on the Jungnangcheon and Tancheon embankments and move among Ttukseom, the Han River riverside, the underground drainage pipes at Yeouido, and station vent rooms. |
| Food | They eat anything: insects at the water’s edge, fish, frogs, fruit, rats, carcasses, and food people have thrown away. In winter, food scraps and the film of food in pipes make up a large share. |
| Breeding and life cycle | In spring a female bears four to eight young. The young become independent in autumn. In winter the family stays in a single burrow, and if the burrow floods they move to a surface alley. If the burrow keeps flooding, the young do not survive. |
| Senses | Smell and hearing are good, and with their whiskers and soles they feel air and water flow in narrow gaps. They sense in their bodies that water in a pipe is rising before rain and move to higher ground. Their eyesight is good in the dark. They cannot read machine signals. |
| Family behavior | The oldest female has priority in moving the young but does not command a fight. Several families share the same pipe by taking turns. If one family disappears, a neighboring family takes over the burrow. |
| Disease and danger | They carry rabies, canine distemper, mange, and parasites. Raccoon dogs with mange lose their fur, starve, and come near people. Parasite eggs such as those of Echinococcus pass through droppings onto water and vegetables and infect people. On the Government side with no doctors, an outbreak is hard to stop once it spreads. |
| Response principles | Fit wire mesh over vents and drains, keep food in lidded containers, and clear sediment from drainage pipes on time. If poison bait is used, carcasses rot and pollute the water downstream, and people and livestock fall sick first. Results are judged not by how many were caught but by the number of times they entered underground spaces and the number of people who fell sick. |

### G04E01 · Burrow family

- Size and place: They live in riverbank burrows, under manholes, and in drainage pipes.
- Winter behavior: The oldest female moves the young and goes first through narrow passages. Before rain she moves the young to higher vent rooms.
- Danger: At night they circle within fifty meters of an exit and raid food-waste yards.
- Response: Fit wire mesh over vents and drains and close food containers. Do not disturb a burrow with young in it.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0045"></a>
<a id="g04e02"></a>

### G04E02 · Pipe-patrolling individual

- Size and place: It patrols the pipes at Ttukseom and the Yeouido drainage pipes.
- Winter behavior: By day it sleeps in the pipes and at night patrols near the pipe exits.
- Danger: It eats sediment and blocks the way water flows, and bites a person who goes down to inspect.
- Response: Go down in pairs to inspect the pipes. Close off the entrances so burrows cannot be made inside.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0078"></a>
<a id="g04e03"></a>

### G04E03 · Night-patrolling individual

- Size and place: Alleys within fifty meters of a station exit.
- Winter behavior: After dark they circle the alleys looking for rubbish, food, and rats. They avoid people.
- Danger: They rummage through food bins and chicken coops in front of houses.
- Response: Do not leave food outside at night.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0094"></a>
<a id="g04e07"></a>

### G04E07 · Young

- Size and place: They stay in the burrow with the mother.
- Winter behavior: Many die in the first winter. If the burrow floods, the mother moves them.
- Danger: When people see them and take them in to raise, the people catch the disease.
- Response: Do not bring the young home. Diseases can pass to you.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0102"></a>
<a id="g04e09"></a>

### G04E09 · Mange-ridden individual

- Size and place: It circles alleys and the edges of underground spaces.
- Winter behavior: Its fur has fallen out and it is thin and weak to the cold. It comes close looking for food people have thrown away.
- Danger: Mange and distemper pass to dogs and people.
- Response: Do not approach, and report it. Wash any tools that touched it.

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0114"></a>
<a id="g04e12"></a>

### G04E12 · Old raccoon dog

- Size and place: Close to pipe entrances and exits.
- Winter behavior: Its teeth are worn and it cannot run well, so it stays beside rubbish heaps.
- Danger: If approached it bites in defense.
- Response: Clear the rubbish heaps so there is no food.

## Habitat and Winter Response

A raccoon dog’s territory is set by waterside and pipes. The great habitats are Ttukseom in Seongdong-gu and the Jungnangcheon embankment in Jungnang-gu on Government land, the underground drainage pipes of Yeouido in Yeongdeungpo-gu in the Free City, and the Tancheon embankment in Songpa-gu and Gangnam-gu on Arc land. When snow settles and the streams freeze, they leave their burrows and move to vents on the embankments and underground spaces of stations.

Raccoon dogs enter stations by three routes: vents, gaps where a shutter does not close, and drains. Deep levels are easily blocked by closing shutters and fitting wire mesh over vents. But people who live on the ground floor and above must block holes under walls and gaps in grain stores themselves. Arc has few people watching the station entrances, so fitting mesh over vents is slow there.

Good response means removing food and dens. Close food bins, clear pipe sediment on time, and block burrow entrances on the embankments. Poison bait and full flooding are not used, because carcasses rot and spread disease to the water downstream and to workers. Isolate sick animals and vaccinate the dogs and people who were near them.

Results are not measured by the number caught. Look at the number of times they entered underground spaces, the number of people who fell sick, and how long a pipe was blocked. Every few years, rest an embankment and its pipes so that new burrows are not dug again in the same place.

## Linked scenarios

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0184"></a>

### G04-SC1 · The Leading Indicator Under the Manhole

- Stage: 1
- Trigger: Before sudden thaw makes the water rise, a raccoon dog family moves from under a manhole to a vent room.
- Actors: Free City stationmaster, pipe maintenance workers, ordinary citizens
- Ecological mechanism: Raccoon dogs sense rising water in their bodies first and move to higher ground. This is a real wild-animal habit.
- Choices: Treat the raccoon dogs’ movement as an early warning / Wait for the water-level measurement / Pre-heat the pumps only when the two differ
- Outcomes: An early warning buys preparation time but leaves responsibility for false alarms. Waiting for the measurement means being late for a sudden inflow. Pre-heating only when the two differ saves fuel but misses short downpours.
- Moral cost: The burden of entrusting public safety to animal movements remains.
- Source dossier: G04

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0186"></a>

### G04-SC2 · Two Families in the Dredging Window

- Stage: 2
- Trigger: On the day pipe sediment is cleared, two raccoon dog families were found using burrows in the same pipe at different times of day.
- Actors: Government stationmaster in Seongdong, pipe workers, White Cross
- Ecological mechanism: Several families share the same pipe by taking turns, and if one family disappears a neighboring family takes over the burrow.
- Choices: Clear all the sediment at once / Clear it in sections / Wait for a day the burrows are empty
- Outcomes: Clearing all at once drives families that lost their burrows to the surface market. Sections make the job longer. Waiting for an empty day leaves the pipe blocked longer.
- Moral cost: Decide whether to keep the pipe in order, or to risk families with nowhere to go moving to the market.
- Source dossier: G04

<a id="Hostile-Group-G04-heading-0188"></a>

### G04-SC3 · The Dry Passage of the Pump Room

- Stage: 3
- Trigger: When a cold snap arrives, people and raccoon dogs share a single dry passage to the pump room.
- Actors: Stationmasters of the Free City and the Government, pump maintenance workers, ordinary citizens
- Ecological mechanism: When snow and ice block other routes, both raccoon dogs and people converge on a single dry passage.
- Choices: Make a heated shelter in the passage to lead the raccoon dogs / Postpone maintenance / Lay a temporary footing outside the passage
- Outcomes: A shelter gathers the raccoon dogs in one place but disease may spread. Postponing maintenance delays the pump. Footing is used for people’s safety.
- Moral cost: Pump maintenance, people’s evacuation, and a dry place for the raccoon dogs collide in one passage.
- Source dossier: G04
