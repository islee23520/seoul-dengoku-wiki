## Common species and variants

The dead siding of the Gangnam Line's Changdong depot hummed first, and a few seconds later a metal knee slowly unfolded from deep inside the maintenance shed. Even after the dust settled, the bipedal heavy walker did not walk straight off. It pressed the floor with the pressure grid of its soles, lowered the counterweight behind its body, and proclaimed its work radius with three low-frequency roars.

The Iron Dinosaur was not a living ancient organism but a lineage of pre-collapse work machines. The G26E identifiers marked machine types and derivatives split from existing chassis forms, and meant neither biological breeding nor natural evolution.

### G26E01 · Load-Jawed Walker

- Source batch: M041

- Role and body: A bipedal heavy-duty vanguard whose two thick legs and rearward counterweight give it a dinosaur-like outline. Its jaw-shaped load clamp lifts debris a little at a time to make room for the towing equipment that follows.
- Origin: A pre-collapse structural-repair advance unit was secured in the Gangnam Line depot maintenance shed. When inspection power returned, it resumed the support checks interrupted at the time of the collapse.
- Habitat and needs: It picks its footing along undercarriage work lines and the edges of subsided concrete. Its high-output battery pack is finite; without knee hydraulic seals and sole load pads, it cannot stand.
- Life cycle and maintenance: Before departure, it hooks its tail counterweight to a maintenance support and measures knee leakage. Joints that exceed the limit lock, so an old unit drags one foot rather than venture beyond the work line.
- Behavior and hierarchy: It passes only sections with confirmed floor bearing capacity to the towing units behind it. If it misses a void beneath a thin work platform, a platform it has judged safe can collapse with people on it.
- Escalation: At stage 1, test footfalls repeat; at stage 2, temporary platforms lift. At stage 3, its large legs and counterweight block a narrow work line where load testing has begun, splitting the evacuation column.
- Engagement and response: Have it rest its clamp on the depot’s independent load-test stand, then secure its knees with maintenance latches. Blowing out an ankle sends the toppling counterweight into the rescue crew behind it, so merely knocking it down is not a victory.
- Negotiation and moral choice: Given a human-verified bearing-capacity chart and a share of charging power, it can help lift debris. Patient-transport carts need the same power used for a single heavy lift, so record which rescue takes priority in the ledger.

- Role and body: A four-legged towing unit with a triangular yoke projecting ahead of its head; the three horn-like anchor points and broad neck shield resemble a large ancient reptile. This first elite unit’s task is not to pursue enemies but to tow heavy frames continuously.
- Origin: Three-point fastening equipment used to transport reinforcement before the collapse was released from a long-term seal on a Gwangjin Line depot siding. The building at its destination is gone, but a transport-completion signal was never entered.
- Habitat and needs: It stays in siding loading yards with enough room to turn its neck shield. It must actually be supplied with wire drums, towing pins, and gear oil, and the heavier its load, the more often it returns to a charging support.
- Life cycle and maintenance: If the three towlines have different tensions, it winds in the slackest first to equalize them. A unit with broken drum teeth is downgraded to light work using two lines; a person must install a new drum to restore its rated capacity.
- Behavior and hierarchy: It carries frames according to the bearing-capacity chart left by the lead walker. It treats everything attached to its three rings as the same cargo, so even when a resident’s awning post is caught, it cannot read the ownership mark.
- Escalation: A test pull on the anchor points is the stage 1 warning. At stage 2, posts in living quarters are dragged toward the siding; stage 3 begins when the three lines block the passage so no one can approach.
- Engagement and response: First take the cargo’s weight on auxiliary supports, then apply the drum’s maintenance brake. Cutting a taut wire first whips its freed end across at head height.
- Negotiation and moral choice: Inserting a completion pin in the destination support can end one transport contract. A decision must be made whether to keep the recovered reinforcement for the next rescue job or return it to residents whose materials were taken; stopping the unit does not settle the ownership dispute.

- Role and body: A second elite monitor with rangefinders beneath long, outstretched pterosaur-like wings. The wings serve as both lifting surfaces and measurement baselines; after hovering briefly, it perches on a high maintenance stand and reports crack locations to the heavy units on the ground.
- Origin: A pre-collapse aerial surveying line that inspected Gangnam Line roof trusses and Gwangjin Line siding reinforcements survived. Once granted access to its sealed maintenance stand, it resumed surveying against the outline of a roof that had already been removed.
- Habitat and needs: Its operating area is the upper part of a depot, where air flows and it can spread both wings. It uses propulsion power and wing tension lines and needs a clean rangefinder window; it cannot fly into narrow underground conduits.
- Life cycle and maintenance: Each time it lands, it aligns its left and right wingtips with reference grooves on the support. If one wingtip fails to reach its groove, it stops flying and performs only fixed-position monitoring, leaving broad blind spots in the damaged unit’s observations.
- Behavior and hierarchy: It does not directly command towing direction; it marks hazardous load zones. If it mistakes rainwater reflections or hanging tarps for cracks, the heavy units clear work crews even from beneath sound roofs.
- Escalation: Surveying light cast on the wrong place signals stage 1. At stage 2, the repair queue shifts to empty zones; at stage 3, overlapping hazard markings cause every route across the depot to be treated as a no-load zone.
- Engagement and response: Set up a frame measured on site beside the maintenance stand and clean the rangefinder window to expose the error. Shooting it down in flight drops its wings and battery onto the work line, so capture using the landing latch is preferable.
- Negotiation and moral choice: Trade survey commissions for each inspection section for wing-repair parts. Adopting a new reference allows the old hazard map to be discarded, but also exposes roof routes residents have kept hidden, making protection of their evacuation routes a separate issue.
- Scenario links: G26-SC1, G26-SC2, G26-SC3.

- Negotiation and moral choice: Agreeing that it will protect only fall zones confirmed by direct measurement yields a safe passage. This creates the burden of dividing recovered armor between residential roofs and mechanical shields.

## Habitat record

The dead siding of the Changdong rolling-stock depot on the Gangnam Line rang first, and a few seconds later a metal knee slowly straightened deep inside the maintenance shed.

Even after the dust had fallen away, the semi-upright heavy machine did not walk at once.

It pressed the floor with its foot-pressure grid, lowered the counterweight behind its body, and proclaimed its working radius with three low-frequency alarms.

In front of it, low, wide triangular tow machines raised their shield plates at a slant, and the long-wing fixed-wing watchers that had been tied to the ceiling launchers prepared for a short takeoff run.

The sound the people on site called the fossil roar was not the cry of a predator but the work alarm of heavy machinery that believed its work was not yet finished.

Before the collapse this line had been a heavy-transport platform modeled on the posture and skeleton of ancient reptiles.

The bipedal semi-upright machines climbed over debris, the triangular units handled towing and shielding against falling objects, and the long-wing units drew safe work polygons from above.

They went to sleep as complete formations at the Changdong rolling-stock depot siding on the Gangnam Line and the Godeok base on the Gwangjin Line and restarted generations later, but all that later generations could read was part of the axle-load tables and boundary signals.

The design intent that would have explained why some walls were marked for reinforcement and others for demolition had vanished.

Their present behavior was not an autonomous doctrine of war but a routine in which the work order from before the sealing had hardened, and because of this incomplete legibility they were classed in the lineage of lost technology.

The Iron Dinosaur was large but not all-powerful.

The semi-upright machines exerted force only on floors whose loads had been verified, and the triangular units could not change the angle of their shields without turning room.

The long-wing units were weak against snow and smoke and had to return to their tow launchers after short flights.

The ground formations did not take in new areas without an aerial map.

Foot pressure and joint torque read structural vibration well but could not tell a person inside a tent from an empty screen, and could mistake a stopped vehicle for reinforcement to be moved.

Even the load model F10 made could not see as far as old underground piping, so HC17 technicians had to set test weights and confirm the real deflection.

Hydraulic oil and intact joints were the food of this ecology.

The maintenance shed's traction batteries and regenerative braking power raised the bodies, but winter preheating alone cut working hours sharply.

When a single knee bearing wore out, the whole semi-upright machine lost its standing as the reference body, and when the tow cables wore down, the triangular units remained only as shields.

Two stopped units could be combined to revive one, but no stronger power core could be made.

When HC17 supplied rail steel and fuel, the heavy convoy route opened, but the same resources were needed to repair civilian trains and the water-supply equipment of the Gwangjin Line.

The decision to preserve the formation was not a matter of gaining enormous power but of paying for hydraulic oil and floor verification with every job.

Its life began with a hydraulic charge and a single aerial survey.

When the long-wing units drew a polygon, the triangular tow machines made a line of retreat, and the semi-upright machine entered last as the range of activity spread.

When the map and the pressure values disagreed, the formation stalled, pushing the same debris and pulling it back.

At that moment, presenting the real load table and boundary stakes could bring it down to a negotiable work group.

Breaking it up was carried out not by demolition but in this order: recovering the long-wing units, detaching the tow lines, locking the knees, and cooling the power core.

Keeping to this order left room to leave the shield plates and towing capacity to civilian infrastructure.

### G26-SC1 · The Third Stamp on the Siding

When the reference body that woke in the Gangnam Line maintenance shed stamped its foot a third time, the cavity beneath the old siding shook.

HC17 wanted to use the units at once for towing freight cars in the north, but F10's map showed abnormal deflection under only one foot.

The people on site could set test weights at several positions to lead the reference body onto a safe rail, lay load-spreading plates to lock its knees in place, or accept the risk and order a short tow.

Choosing to investigate delayed the fuel wagons from XT04, and hasty operation could sink the siding and the sealed vault together.

### G26-SC2 · The Pipe the Shields Dragged Out

At the Godeok base of the Gwangjin Line, triangular tow machines clearing a collapsed barrier wall judged the guard of a water-supply pipe to be waste reinforcement.

The eastern convoy route widened, but the vibration of the pipe behind the shields grew, and the semi-upright machine tried to enter within the old work boundary drawn by the long-wing units.

The people on site could set new boundary stakes and tow only the barrier wall in sections, halt the convoy for a day and build pipe supports first, or accept the water-supply loss and open the way in one go.

HC17's supply promise and the Gwangjin Line's water supply hung on the two ends of the same tow line, and a delay on either side passed its cost to another state.

### G26-SC3 · The Cooling Core Inside the Roar Wall

When the long-wing watchers could not return in a blizzard, the formations of the Gangnam Line siding and the Gwangjin Line base each held a different safety polygon.

The triangular units blocked the entrance with a continuous shield wall, and the semi-upright reference body held out behind them, guarding its overheated power core.

The people on site had to recover the watchers with landing markers and reconcile the maps, hang no-load pulleys on each shield and release the tension in order, or connect external cooling pipes and then lock the reference body's knees.

Blowing up the core opened the passage fastest, but the shock reached even the fuel and water-supply facilities.

The ending that saved the formation required public load tests and joint supervision for every heavy job from then on, and the ending that dismantled it gave parts at once but left the next large piece of collapse debris to be moved by human hands.
