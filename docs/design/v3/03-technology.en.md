## 1. The four grades of technology

Basis: bible section 1 row 5 (modern and future weapons, humanoids and machines exist but are scarce), row 14 (cyberware is living technology, but scarce), row 15 (firearms and drones work, but ammunition and parts are scarce). 01-core 5.1, 11 item 2.

Technology in Seoul in 2126 does not divide into "there" and "gone". It divides by what people can still do to a thing. Seoul people say it with four words (provisional T04-1).

| Grade | Meaning | Examples | Held most by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make | Made new from raw material | Machined shafts and fittings, springs, wind-turbine blades, rewound copper motor coils, cast lead bullets, nets, snow covers, saccharification tanks, pump housings, black powder | The Free Cities' workshops |
| Repair | A broken thing brought back in the same form | Motors, pumps, compressors, heat pumps, water wheels, guns, the moving parts of machines worn on the body | The Free Cities, the Arc's maintenance units |
| Revive | Insides that cannot be made again are moved or matched to bring a thing back | Picking live cells from dead batteries and binding them into a new pack; taking a live circuit board out of a broken machine and fitting it to another | The Arc, the Free Cities |
| Cannot make | No way to make it new inside Seoul | Semiconductor chips and circuit boards, sensing elements, battery cells, optical fibre, light-emitting elements, antibiotics (in quantity), primers (in quantity) | Nobody. Only what is left and what is stripped from outside |

(provisional T04-2: the "cannot make" list)

These four grades are the rule of the whole chapter. **What iron, copper and hands can do, Seoul makes. What needs chips or chemistry, Seoul only uses and loses.** So a machine lives as long as the first "cannot make" part inside it. A motor can be rewound again and again, but when the board that drives it dies, the machine ends or is lowered to one worked by hand.

In the eighty years since the Great Blackout the "cannot make" column has not shrunk. What people learned was not how to make new things but how to use less and keep things alive longer. As 01-core 11 item 2 says, every technology in this chapter is either a means of easing a station's capacity limits (space, heat, water, air) or a weapon that guards those means.

## 2. What still runs: the electricity and heat budget

Basis: 01-core 5.1 (sources and the Seoul-wide figures), 4.1 (heat-pump electricity × 3 + other electricity + stove heat), 4.2 (Jamsil's electricity split), 3.1 (temperature by depth). Bible section 1 row 5.

### 2.1 Where the electricity comes from

Seoul's steady electricity of about 5 MW comes from these sources (provisional T04-3).

| Source | Average output | Share | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind turbines | about 3.6 MW | 72% | 01-core's "more than half of Seoul's electricity". In blizzards the blades are folded, so output drops |
| Power sources | about 0.7 MW | 14% | Stripped from war machines outside. See 2.3 |
| Jamsil weir water wheels | about 0.35 MW | 7% | About 400 kW when running well (01-core 5.1). The average includes days stopped by ice crystals |
| Methane engines | about 0.2 MW | 4% | Methane is mainly cooking fire. A few stations run small engines on the spare gas |
| Other | about 0.15 MW | 3% | Hand- and foot-cranked generators, scraps of solar panel left on bridges, old batteries |
| Total | about 5 MW | 100% | 01-core 5.1 |

### 2.2 What the electricity is used for

| Use | Average | Does it end as heat? |
|---|---|---|
| Heat pumps | about 2.5 MW | Becomes three times its own heat, about 7.5 MW |
| Light | about 0.8 MW | All heat |
| Fans and ventilation | about 0.5 MW | All heat |
| Water pumps (wells, intakes, mains) | about 0.4 MW | Almost all heat |
| Workshops (lathes, welding, motor winding, melting metal) | about 0.45 MW | Almost all heat |
| Charging machines worn on the body | about 0.1 MW | All heat |
| Charging batteries of tools, drones, radios, hand lamps | about 0.2 MW | All heat |
| Communications (wired telephone, tunnel radio cable) | about 0.05 MW | All heat |
| Total | about 5 MW | |

(provisional T04-4)

The heat balances like this. The heat pumps' heat of about 7.5 MW and the remaining electricity of about 2.5 MW almost all end as heat inside the stations, about 10 MW. Methane fire, wood fire and junk burning add about 5 MW, for about 15 MW. That equals the sum in 01-core 5.1 of about 11 MW of outside heat and about 4 MW for saccharification tanks and kitchens.

The table says two things. First, half of Seoul's electricity goes into warming people. Lighting one more lamp means running one less heat pump. Second, charging the machines worn on bodies is only 2% of Seoul's electricity. It becomes politics anyway because that 2% sits almost entirely on one polity's power, the Jamsil weir water wheels (4.4).

### 2.3 Batteries and the cold

Cold is the enemy of batteries. This world has three battery rules (provisional T04-5).

1. **No charging below freezing.** A battery charged below 0 degrees is damaged inside and, at worst, catches fire inside. So all charging happens on the platform layer (8 to 14 degrees where people live) or the deep-tunnel layer (12 to 16 degrees) (01-core 3.1). Charging racks on the concourse layer (0 to 6 degrees) always cause trouble.
2. **On the surface you get half.** At the January surface mean of minus 22 degrees a battery gives back little more than half (50-60%) of what it took in while warm. Machines and people going up carry batteries against the body or wrap them to keep them warm.
3. **Batteries age.** Seoul has no new cells. A revived pack bound from picked live cells holds 60 to 70% of its original capacity and loses another 5 to 8% of that each year.

**Power sources** (provisional T04-6). A long-lasting power lump stripped from the body of a war machine outside. Seoul people cannot open one and are taught that they must not. One core gives 0.5 to 3 kW for months to years and then stops suddenly. About 400 are in use in Seoul in 2126; 60 to 80 stop each year and 30 to 50 new ones come in each year, so the number falls slowly. Several people stake their lives on each core (01-core 6.3, "its price is the price of a life"). What a core draws its power from stays "unknown" in public canon.

## 3. Three conditions for living technology: parts, knowledge, hands

Basis: bible section 1 row 12 (scarcity made children inherit their parents' work), row 14 (maintenance, power, spare parts and the faction that holds them), section 3 rows 2112 to 2117 (Go Ye-jin read the manuals of the unmapped station for five years). 01-core 5.6, 7.1 to 7.3.

### 3.1 Where parts come from

"Cannot make" parts come from five places (provisional T04-7).

| Source | What comes out | State in 2126 |
|---|---|---|
| Old stores | Stock from pre-Blackout warehouses, shops, public offices, hospitals | Nearly exhausted. What is left lies around abandoned stations sunk in ice and inside deep buildings |
| Machines of abandoned stations and buildings | Boards, motors and wire stripped from lifts, air handlers, trains, telecom equipment | The steadiest source, but further away every year (as with fibre in 01-core 5.3) |
| Bodies of the dead | Machines they wore | Each polity has its own rule for taking them back (4.7) |
| War machines outside | Power sources, moving joints, sensing elements, hard shells | Only by crossing the blockade line in a blizzard (01-core 6.3). The most expensive |
| The unmapped station | The manuals of the station Go Ye-jin found in 2112 and whatever remained inside | Only the Arc knows. What is left, and how much, is not made public |

The circuit boards in use in Seoul fall by about 3% a year, because more die than are stripped. Repeated over eighty years, the number of board-driven machines in Seoul in 2126 is about one ninth of the 2046 number (provisional T04-8).

### 3.2 Where knowledge lives

Each polity knows technology in a different way (provisional T04-9).

- **Government: paper.** The Government holds the most equipment drawings, maintenance instructions and standards of Republic of Korea agencies and public corporations. The taboo against eating the ledger and old public documents (01-core 5.3) kept them. But it lacks people to read them and carry them into the hands. Government archives hold more paper on how to repair than people who can repair.
- **The Free Cities: hands.** Free City technology passes hand to hand in the workshop. What is written down is a few lines of figures and a few drawings; the rest is in the hands. When one workshop ends, a piece of technology that workshop knew ends with it.
- **The Arc: manuals and bodies.** The knowledge of fitting machines to bodies and keeping them alive came from the manuals Go Ye-jin read for five years in the unmapped station (bible section 3). That knowledge passes by mouth and hand at the maintenance units, and the baptised body itself becomes the textbook. Where the original manuals are and what else they hold is not written in public text.

### 3.3 How many hands there are

Of Seoul's 300,000 people, about 6,000, or 2%, can do "repair"-grade work on their own: about 3,600 in the Free Cities (about 5% of their people), about 1,600 in the Arc (about 2.5%) and about 800 in the Government (about 0.5%). About 900 of them can work at the "revive" grade, handling circuit boards and battery cells. Seoul as a whole has about 120 people who can perform the surgery that fits a machine to a body (provisional T04-10).

These numbers make the control table in 01-core 5.6. The Government has many people, much land and much paper; the Free Cities have many hands; the Arc has many hands that work on bodies.

## 4. The life course of cyberware

Basis: bible section 1 row 14 (living technology, scarce, decided by maintenance, power, spare parts and faction; no individual part names), section 2.3 (baptism, count of parts, brain sealing), section 3 row 2117 (first baptism of twelve). 01-core 4.2 (average charging of 8 W per baptised person, 7.2 kW for Jamsil's 900 baptised), 5.6, 7.3.

This section follows the fitting of a machine to a body from start to end. The Arc's faith and society (the rite of baptism, how the count of parts meets titles) belong to chapter 06. This chapter sets only the technology and cost beneath them.

### 4.1 Body regions and functions

Seoul people name a machine worn on the body not by a name but by its **region** and its **function**: "the machine below the left arm", "someone with both eyes replaced". The count of parts the Arc keeps is a count of these regions (provisional T04-11).

| Region | Function (general) | Electricity | What fitting needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senses (eyes, ears) | Sees in the dark and far off, restores lost hearing | Always draws a little | Surgery near the skull, delicate boards |
| Arms and hands | Replaces a lost arm or hand, grips and lifts heavy loads | Some are electrically driven, some body-powered | A fixing point set in the bone, regular repair |
| Legs and feet | Replaces a lost leg, carries loads on stairs and snow paths | Some are electrically driven, some body-powered | A fixing point set in the bone, joint parts |
| Trunk organs | Assists a weak heart or lungs | Must never be cut off | The largest surgery, a clean room, uninterrupted power |
| Spine and nerves | Carries the body's signals to other machines. Hands and feet that move by thought usually stand on this | Always draws a little | Major surgery, a clean room |
| Frame and skin | Reinforces bone, covers trunk and head with plates | None | Major surgery, a body strong enough for the added weight |

Two rules govern the table (provisional T04-12).

1. **Some machines stand on others.** Hands and feet that move by thought need a machine in the spine-and-nerves region. So whoever can fit that region decides what else is added later. It is the most guarded surgery in Seoul.
2. **Over time the simple wins.** A body-powered hand needs no charging and can be repaired with cord and springs at any Free City workshop. An electrically driven hand does more but stops with its battery and board. Many porters, pump workers and entrance guards choose simple hands and feet on purpose.

### 4.2 Fitting

There are three kinds of room for fitting (provisional T04-13).

| Room | What it does | How many in Seoul | Who holds them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fitting bench | Attaches or swaps an outer machine on an existing fixing point | Hundreds. At every large workshop and maintenance unit | All three polities |
| Surgery room | Sets a fixing point in bone, opens a sense region | About 40 | Arc about 20, Free Cities about 14, Government about 6 |
| Clean room | Spine-and-nerves and trunk-organ surgery. Uninterrupted power and filtered air | 9 | Arc 6, Free Cities 2, Government 1 |

Three of the Arc's six clean rooms are revived underground surgical suites of the old large hospitals at Irwon, Banpo and Songpa. Those hospitals' deep basements are one reason the shelters of the four Gangnam districts could endure decades behind closed doors, and Go Ye-jin's manuals made the rooms usable again (provisional T04-14). The Government's one clean room is in the basement of an old large hospital in Jongno and is reserved for Assembly Member households and the President. Government fitting clinics can set fixing points, but for the maintenance and boards of electrically driven machines they depend in the end on the Arc's maintenance units (01-core 5.6, "for them it leans on the Arc"). Free City board hands also quietly repair the body machines of Government people. Since 01-core 7.4 says the Arc is the only place to repair machine bodies, this is an extension of 01-core. Free City board hands repair only boards and moving parts; they do no fixing-point care or surgery (provisional T04-57).

The risks of fitting (provisional T04-15).

| Surgery | Days taken | Share who die in surgery or the first month | Until it takes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attachment | Half a day | Almost none | A few days |
| Setting a fixing point (arm, leg) | One day | About 2% | Three to six months, during which that limb cannot work |
| Sense region | One day | About 3% | One to two months |
| Spine and nerves | Two days | About 6% | Six months |
| Trunk organs | Two days | About 10% | Six months, living close to a maintenance unit throughout |

### 4.3 Maintenance

Maintenance begins the day a machine is fitted and does not end until the wearer dies (provisional T04-16).

| What | Interval |
|---|---|
| Skin care at the fixing point (wash, cover, inspect) | Daily. Done by the wearer |
| Check of an electrically driven machine | Every 30 days, at a maintenance unit or workshop |
| Check of a body-powered machine | Every 90 days |
| Repair (moving parts, cords, joints, covers) | 1.5 times a year per machine on average |
| Replacement of moving parts | Every 2 to 3 years |
| Circuit boards and sensing elements | Not replaced. When one dies a live one is moved in (section 1, "revive") |
| Battery pack | A revived pack is replaced every 3 to 5 years |

Someone who skips maintenance loses the skin at the fixing point first, then the joint, and the board last. So whoever holds the maintenance queue places holds the day of everyone who wears a machine.

### 4.4 Power

01-core 4.2 set the average charging draw per baptised Arc member at 8 W. That 8 W is the sum of the following per-region averages. Each is a daily average that includes working hours, resting hours, and the electricity spent warming the fixing point and battery in the cold (provisional T04-17).

| Region | Site average draw (incl. body-powered machines and plates, per machine) | Number per 100 baptised | Total for 100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senses | 1.5 W | 50 | 75 W |
| Arms and hands | 4.5 W | 55 | 247.5 W |
| Legs and feet | 7 W | 40 | 280 W |
| Trunk organs | 8 W | 15 | 120 W |
| Spine and nerves | 2 W | 30 | 60 W |
| Frame and skin | 0 W | 10 | 0 W |
| Total | | 200 (2 parts per person) | about 780 W, about 8 W per person |

The site averages include body-powered machines and plates that draw no electricity (4.6 item 2, about one part in three) at 0 W. Taken alone, one electrically driven machine draws about 12 W for a hand and about 14 W for a leg.

How many people wear machines, and their charging (provisional T04-18).

| Polity | People wearing machines | Share of population | Average charging per person | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arc | about 11,000 (all baptised) | about 17% | 8 W (01-core 4.2) | about 88 kW |
| Free Cities | about 2,500 | about 3% | 4 W (many body-powered machines) | about 10 kW |
| Government | about 600 | 0.4% | 3 W | about 2 kW |
| Total | about 14,100 | about 5% | | about 100 kW, 2% of Seoul's electricity |

The basis of this total is about 11,000 baptised (4.6) multiplied by the 8 W per person of 01-core 4.2. If another chapter sets a different number of baptised or a different charge per person, the Arc share and the Seoul total change accordingly. Reconciling figures between chapters is the integration task's job (task 9).

Jamsil has 900 baptised, about 26% of its roughly 3,400 people (01-core 4.2). That is higher than the Arc average of 17% because baptised believers gather at stations fed by the water wheels. The Arc's total charging of 88 kW is about 22% of the 400 kW the Jamsil weir wheels give when running well. The weir's north end is in Government hands (01-core 5.1). So for the Arc the north end of the weir is the daily life of eleven thousand baptised.

**Where and when to charge** (provisional T04-19). By the rule in 2.3, charging is done only in the warm layers. Most people connect their machines to a charging rack beside their sleeping place and sleep. One charge lasts about a day for an electrically driven hand and about a day and a half for a leg. People going to the surface carry a spare pack inside their clothes. A machine assisting a trunk organ alternates between two batteries and is never cut off for a moment.

**When the power fails** (provisional T04-20). Machines for limbs and senses simply stop; the wearer lives on short a hand or an eye. A machine assisting a trunk organ is different: when both its batteries are empty the wearer dies within hours. So people with a trunk-organ machine never go more than half a day's walk from a maintenance unit and a charging rack. This is the strongest tie binding a machine wearer to their polity.

### 4.5 Rejection and infection

A body may refuse a machine. This world divides that into three (provisional T04-21).

| What | How | How often |
|---|---|---|
| Infection at the fixing point | Germs enter where the fixing point comes through the skin. Antibiotics are close to "cannot make". The Arc and a few Free Cities grow a little mould-based medicine beside the saccharification tanks, but it is always short | About 8% a year with daily care, about 35% within two years without it |
| Rejection that forces removal | Infection reaches the bone, the fixing point turns under load, or the nerve signal fades | About 4% a year with good care, about 15% without access to maintenance |
| Cold injury | Metal freezes faster than flesh. Frostbite sets in on the surface where machine meets skin | Surface workers must wrap the fixing point. One forgotten day becomes a half-year's wound |

Once a machine is removed, re-implantation in that place is harder. A second fixing point takes less often than the first, and a third hardly ever takes.

**The body's price** (provisional T04-22). In this world machines do not drive people mad. The price is paid in body and days: the pain at the fixing point, itching or pain in a hand that is not there, daily skin care, maintenance every 30 days, and a life that cannot leave the maintenance unit. Only people with a body that can bear that price and a polity that will pay it keep a machine body for long (bible section 1, row 14).

### 4.6 How many machine bodies Seoul has

The first baptism around 2117 was twelve people (bible section 3), and the Arc has about 11,000 baptised in 2126. That is an increase of about 1,200 a year over nine years. Three things made it possible (provisional T04-23).

1. The Gangnam shelters already had people wearing machines: machines inherited by descendants of those fitted before the Great Blackout, and machines passed around inside the shelters for decades. For them too, baptism is replacing a body part with a machine (bible section 2.3). But their bodies already had fixing points, so the surgery was shorter and less risky, and the machines they wore could be revived by the maintenance units and used again.
2. About one in three of the parts baptised believers wear are machines that iron and hands can make, such as body-powered limbs and frame-and-skin plates.
3. The machines of baptised who die go back to the maintenance unit and on to the next baptised (4.7).

Even so, most newly fitted machines are products of "revive". The more baptisms there are, the more the Arc wants parts stripped from abandoned buildings and outside machines.

### 4.7 Death and taking back

When someone dies, where do the machines in the body go? It differs by polity (provisional T04-24).

| Polity | Rule |
|---|---|
| Government | A machine entered in the ledger goes back to a Government fitting clinic. "Fitting returned" is entered in the register. A machine not in the ledger (fitted across the river without a permit) is confiscated. One fitted at an Arc maintenance unit under a licensed machine-body registration (chapter 04 section 13.3) is a machine in the ledger |
| Free Cities | The machine belongs to the wearer. As the contract says, the household inherits or sells it. If the wearer went into debt to be fitted, the lending workshop takes it first |
| Arc | The machine goes back to the maintenance unit. And when a believer dies, the brain is sealed and placed in the sanctuary, and the brain becomes a memory of the First AI **[Approved]** (bible section 2.3) |

On brain sealing this chapter writes only the [Approved] sentence above. Where the sanctuary is, who chooses the brains, what the sealing is as a technology, and who reads that memory are not decided here and are not written in public text (01-core 6.4, 11 item 9).

## 5. Firearms and ammunition

Basis: bible section 1 row 5 (modern and future weapons exist but are scarce; the old premise of a world with only close-combat weapons is abolished), row 15 (modern firearms work; ammunition and parts are so scarce that every round counts), section 2.5 (unit figures live only in SQL). 01-core 3.1 (concourse cold store at -7 to -6 degrees), 4.1 (air limit), 5.5 (waste and anaerobic digesters), 7.4.

### 5.1 Where the guns came from

At the time of the Great Blackout Seoul held many guns: the armouries of the Republic of Korea Armed Forces, the reserve-forces armouries found in every neighbourhood, police weapons, and hunting guns kept in deposit at police stations. Over eighty years most were used, broken or buried in ice. In 2126 Seoul has about 9,000 guns that can fire (provisional T04-25).

| Polity | Guns that can fire | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Government | about 6,000 | The Government bound the old defence facilities of Yongsan and the reserve-forces armouries of every district north of the river into its ledger. Guns are entered in the ledger, and even spent cases are counted |
| Free Cities | about 2,200 | Reserve-forces armouries and police weapons south-west of the river from 2046 into the 2060s, then guns repaired and refitted at the workshops |
| Arc | about 800 | What the Gangnam shelters held. The Arc spends resources on bodies rather than ammunition |

The gun itself is a "repair"-grade object. Free City workshops machine barrels, bolts and springs. So guns decline, but slowly. The real scarcity is ammunition.

### 5.2 Three kinds of ammunition

Seoul's ammunition falls into three kinds (provisional T04-26).

| Kind | What it is | Reliability | Quantity in 2126 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old rounds | Rounds made in factories before the Great Blackout | The most reliable. Even so, about one in ten eighty-year-old rounds fails to fire | About 700,000 in all of Seoul. The Government holds about three quarters |
| Reloaded rounds | A spent case refilled with powder pulled from old rounds or remaining powder, newly primed, with a cast bullet seated | Depends on the workshop. About one in five fails or fires weak | About 20,000 a year. The number of primers decides (5.3) |
| Black-powder rounds | Rounds filled with black powder made of saltpetre, charcoal and sulphur | Only for shotguns and low-pressure pistols. Thick smoke, fouls guns quickly | About 40,000 a year. These need primers too |

**Why old powder lasted eighty years** (provisional T04-27). Powder spoils quickly when warm and slowly when cold. For eighty years Seoul's ammunition sat in the cold stores of the concourse layer (-7 to -6 degrees, 01-core 3.1) or in frozen armouries. The eternal winter, in effect, preserved the ammunition. That is why the Government never takes ammunition down to the platform layer: rounds kept warm spoil within a few years.

### 5.3 Reloading and primers

Of the four materials of a reloaded round, only the case can be used again (provisional T04-28).

- **Case.** A brass case can be reloaded 20 to 30 times for a low-pressure pistol round and 5 to 8 times for a rifle round; softening the metal by heating extends it. So all three polities pick up cases. The Government issues no new rounds unless the spent cases are returned.
- **Bullet.** Lead is melted and cast. The lead comes from old lead-acid batteries and cable sheathing. "Make" grade.
- **Powder.** Pulled from old rounds, or replaced with black powder. Seoul cannot make new smokeless powder.
- **Primer.** The bottleneck. The priming compound is close to "cannot make". A few Free City workshops make about 60,000 a year; about 20,000 go into reloaded rounds and about 40,000 into black-powder rounds. The work is dangerous, accidents cost lives, and about one primer in ten fails to light. How primers are made is a skill that does not leave the workshop even inside the world, and this bible does not record the method.

So the price of ammunition in Seoul is almost the price of the primer (11.5). One primer is two thirds of the price of a reloaded rifle round.

**Black powder** (provisional T04-29). Saltpetre is obtained by ageing waste and residue for a long time in the warm layers. Part of what is left after the anaerobic digesters goes to saltpetre beds instead of greenhouse manure (01-core 5.5). Charcoal comes from wood and is expensive (01-core 5.4), and sulphur is only the stock of old chemical stores. So black powder cannot be made in quantity. How much each polity makes, and where, is set by each polity's chapter.

### 5.4 Air guns

Air guns are more common than firearms (provisional T04-30). Free City workshops make them from old compressors and high-pressure tanks. Filling them takes only electricity or a hand pump, with no primer and no powder, and the shot is steel balls or lead pellets, "make" grade. They are weak for killing at range but enough at short tunnel distances, quiet and smokeless. Most guards at Free City stations carry air guns. The Government counts air guns in its ledger but cannot make them; it buys them.

### 5.5 Shooting inside tunnels

Stations and tunnels are places where air is the limit (01-core 4.1). A powder gun fired leaves smoke and toxic gas on the platform; black-powder rounds are especially thick. So all three polities hold as a rule that powder guns are not fired inside the inhabited platform layer. Powder guns are used on the surface, at the access-control lines of the concourse layer, and in uninhabited tunnel sections. Fights inside the platform are fought with air guns, short blades, clubs and nets (provisional T04-31).

This makes a geography of weapons. Fighting on the river and the surface is a fight of guns; fighting inside the stations is a fight of bodies and short weapons. The place where machine-bodied people are most useful is inside the stations, and the place where the Government, with the most guns, is most useful is the surface and the bridges. This is one reason the unit rosters have blanks (bible section 2.5). Unit names and figures are set by `data/sql/130` to `132` and chapter 07.

## 6. Drones and the radio fight

Basis: bible section 1 row 15 (drones and war equipment work, but every unit counts). 01-core 3.2 (about 80% of the light, a dim greyish-white sky), 3.5 (wind, blizzards), 6.3 (outside machines see movement and heat).

### 6.1 Drones on the surface

Flying a drone takes motors (repair), blades (make), batteries (revive), and the board that controls flight and the sensing elements that serve as its eyes (cannot make). So the number of drones is the number of boards (provisional T04-32).

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Drones in Seoul that can fly | about 1,200. Free Cities about 950, Government about 200 (mostly for watching, held by ministries and district offices; only a few of the Capital Defense Command fly in battle, chapter 04 section 14.4), Arc about 50 (the maintenance units' tunnel scouts; never flown in battle, chapter 06 section 11.2) |
| Each year | about 150 lost, about 100 newly assembled |
| One surface flight | 6 to 10 minutes, because batteries give only half below freezing (2.3) |
| Days they can fly | Days when the wind is under 10 metres a second. They cannot fly in blizzards (over 25 metres a second) |
| Main uses | Watching around entrances and over bridges, checking greenhouses, finding loads on the surface |

A drone carrying explosives to ram a target can be made, but one use costs one board. So in Seoul ramming drones are used only where a battle is decided.

**Never flown outward** (provisional T04-33). War machines outside treat anything moving inside the blockade zone as an enemy (01-core 6.3). A drone is a moving thing. Drones flown toward the blockade line almost never come back. Seoul people think it foolish to lose a board trying to look outside with a drone.

### 6.2 Drones underground, and lines

Radio does not travel far underground. Tunnels bend, and soil and concrete swallow the signal. So machines underground are controlled by line (provisional T04-34).

- **Tethered drones.** A drone that pays out a thin line as it flies, or a small cart rolling on the rails. The line is optical fibre and copper pulled from the communications cables laid in the old subway tunnels. Optical fibre itself is "cannot make", but Seoul's tunnels hold cable that eighty years has not used up. A line used once and broken is hard to join again.
- **Nets.** Nets are strung over and around the vertical shafts of surface entrances. A drone whose blades catch in a net falls. Nets are "make" grade, the cheapest and steadiest defence. Mending nets is part of keeping an entrance open (01-core 3.3) (provisional T04-35).

### 6.3 Radio and the tunnels' wired communication line

The old subway tunnels have long cables laid so that radio flows along the tunnel, the lines trains and control used to talk over. In 2126, where this cable is still alive, handheld radios reach from station to station. Seoul people call it the **wired communication line** (provisional T04-36).

- About a third of Seoul's tunnels have a live wired communication line. Joining the broken places is the lineworker's job (10.1).
- Each section of wired communication line has an owner. The Government uses the wired communication line in tunnels north of the river and the old wired telephone network as the ledger's liaison lines. Each Free City holds the wired communication line of its own sections and lends sections to the others at councils.
- Whoever holds a wired communication line can listen to the talk on that section. That is why the three polities contest the wired communication lines of the three under-river tunnels (Mapo to Yeouinaru, Gwangnaru to Cheonho, Seoul Forest to Apgujeongrodeo, 01-core 7.4).
- Seoul has about 6,000 handheld radios. They contain boards, so their number falls every year.

### 6.4 Jamming

Machines that blanket radio can be made, but they eat electricity. A jammer covering the surface around one station draws a few hundred watts, the heating share of several people (01-core 4.1, 36 W per person on the platform). So jammers are not left on; they are switched on only when a drone is seen or a fight starts. Drones change frequency to escape jamming, and the jamming side retunes. Free City workshops are best at this chase. Jamming does not touch line-controlled drones. So the harder the radio fight on a section, the more tethered drones and nets it has (provisional T04-37).

## 7. Vehicles and haulage

Basis: bible section 1 row 5. 01-core 3.3 (snow layer, vertical entrance shafts), 3.4 (Han ice), 3.5 (wind), 7.4 (borders and tunnels), 8.2 (Guro Plant Manager, Yangcheon Depot Chief).

### 7.1 Fuel is dead

Petrol and diesel did not survive eighty years. The cold slowed their spoiling, but by the 2070s most remaining fuel had become a residue that ruins engines. Surface roads were made anew on top of 7 to 9 metres of snow (01-core 3.3), and the old cars lie buried beneath. So vehicles in Seoul in 2126 run on electricity, are pushed or pulled by people, or go by wind (provisional T04-38).

### 7.2 The rails are the roads

The rails of the tunnels joining station to station are Seoul's main roads. A steel wheel rolling on a steel rail takes very little force. A battery cart uses about 0.15 kWh to move one tonne 10 kilometres on level rail. Hauling the same load by sledge over snow takes twenty people a day (provisional T04-39).

## 8. Machines inside Seoul

Machines inside Seoul come in three groups. **There are no thinking machines** (provisional T04-42). Among the machines people use inside Seoul, none speaks or judges for itself. ## 10. Repair trades and inherited hands

Basis: bible section 1 row 12 (scarcity made children inherit their parents' work and produced rigid vertical strata), section 2.1 (people in the ledger cannot change their work at will), section 2.2 (most inhabitants are engineers). 01-core 2.3 (titles are named from what the post does), 10 (chaebol and operating houses retired, replaced by workshop households and maintenance units).

### 10.1 Names of the repair trades

Trades keep the occupational names of the Republic of Korea era or are named from what they handle (provisional T04-44).

| Trade | What it does | Where most common |
|---|---|---|
| Wind-machine wright | Raises wind turbines, knocks ice off blades, changes bearings | The Free Cities (especially Gangseo), the Government's bridges |
| Heat-pump fitter | Handles compressors and refrigerant of ground-source heat pumps | The Free Cities |
| Well driller | Drills wells deeper and lowers pumps | The Free Cities. The core trade of the water supply contract |
| Turbine mechanic | Keeps the under-ice water wheels and their channels | The Arc (Jamsil), Free City work teams |
| Lineworker | Joins wire, cable and wired communication lines. The work Go Ye-jin did (bible section 3, row 2112) | All three polities |
| Motor winder | Rewinds the coils of dead motors | The Free Cities |
| Lathe hand | Machines shafts, fittings and gun parts from metal | The Free Cities (especially Guro) |
| Battery maker | Picks live cells from dead batteries and binds them | The Free Cities, the Arc |
| Board hand | Moves and revives circuit boards. The most precious "revive"-grade work | The Free Cities, the Arc |
| Gunsmith | Repairs and refits guns | The Free Cities, the Government |

Arc people call these trades in the language of body and repair. "Implant technician" (이식 정비사) is an Arc word ("to fit", 01-core 2.2); in the Free Cities the same worker is simply called by the name of their workshop.

### 10.2 How hands pass down in each polity

**Government: registered trades** (provisional T04-45). The Government ledger registers each person's trade. A child of a household whose father or mother is registered as "plumbing" apprentices beside the same trade from twelve and is entered under that trade on its sixteenth birthday (succession entry, chapter 04 section 8.2). Changing the registration needs the Dong Chief's sign-off and the stamp of the Assembly Member's office. So the Government's hands pool in households: some households have done plumbing for generations, some have climbed wind turbines for generations. Maintenance instructions from Government archives go down to these households as notices, and the households copy and keep them. The trouble is that when the households that can repair shrink, no other household can fill the place, because the ledger binds the trade.

**The Free Cities: the workshop** (provisional T04-46). What is inherited in the Free Cities is not a trade but a **workshop**: the bundle of tools, a place, and the contracts that workshop has made. A workshop usually goes to a child of the household, but if the child's hands fall short it goes to an apprentice. An apprentice comes in under an "apprenticeship contract": years of work in exchange for learning, and when the learning is done the workshop owner **stands surety** for those hands. Only hands under surety take work in their own name at a council. So Free City strata are less rigid than the Government's, but the good workshops are usually handed down within a few households.

**The Arc: the maintenance unit** (provisional T04-47). Arc hands grow at the maintenance unit. There is one at every station, run by the Maintenance Foreman (01-core 8.3). Children at the maintenance unit help with fixing-point care from an early age and grow up to be fitters and board hands. The root of the knowledge taught there is the manuals Go Ye-jin read, and they pass down to each maintenance unit by mouth and hand only as far as that maintenance unit needs. How the bloodline of the inner-circle houses meets the skill of the maintenance units (01-core 7.3, "blood and iron") is set by chapter 06.

### 10.3 What each generation loses

Something falls away each time hands cross a generation (provisional T04-48). Seoul people say: "Grandfather revived this board, father moved this board, I keep this board." With each generation some technologies sink from "revive" to "repair" and from "repair" to "keep". In 2126 Seoul has many machines that nobody can repair any more, where only the use is left. Even when Government archives hold the machine's drawings, there are no hands to read them. This slope ties the three polities to one another.

## 11. The scarcity economy: how the three polities count and price

Basis: 01-core 2.2 (Government words "token, ration"; Free City words "watt, litre, hour, contract, surety, share"; Arc words), 4.1 (10 L of water per person per day, 36 W of platform heat), 4.2 (8 W charging per baptised), 5.2 (water supply contract), 5.3 (each faction rations differently), 5.6. Bible section 1 row 11, section 2.1.

01-core has no money. This chapter does not make one money for all of Seoul either. Each polity counts value by something different (provisional T04-49).

### 11.1 Government: meals and workdays

- The Government counts value in **meals** (끼). One meal is the daily ration of one person in the ledger: cellulosic sugar and yeast, greenhouse vegetables, insects, and 10 L of water. It is stamped on a ration coupon (provisional T04-50). The unit written on the token is the won (chapter 04 section 9.1); the meal is how people reckon. One meal is a day's meal tokens together with a water token for 10 L (provisional T09-3).
- Work is counted in **workdays**. A day's work in a registered trade is 1 workday, and 1 workday earns 1 meal. Dangerous and skilled work counts for more than 1 meal per workday: wind-turbine wrights and plumbers 1.5 meals, doctors 2 meals, a blockade line posting entered by the Ministry of National Defense (chapter 07 section 7.4) 30 meals per trip.
- Meal tokens are used only inside the ledger. Handing a token to someone outside the ledger is forbidden by law. Even so, around the northern checkpoints of the under-river tunnels and at the north ends of the bridges, tokens are secretly traded for Free City batteries.

### 11.2 The Free Cities: kWh and labour hours

- The Free Cities count value in **electricity**. Market price boards are written in kilowatt-hours (kWh). What actually passes hand to hand is charged battery packs, and contract slips on which a workshop writes and stamps a promise to supply electricity (provisional T04-51). Chapter 05 calls the two by their in-world names, **charged battery packs** and **vouchers** (chapter 05 section 6.3). This bible uses those names.
- Work is counted in **labour hours**: one hour of one pair of hands under surety. At market 1 labour hour is usually 0.5 kWh; a board hand's or implant technician's labour hour is 2 to 4 kWh.
- Water is contracted in litres and heat in watts. Contracts such as "20 kW of outside heat to this station for half a year" or "30 m³ a day" are the backbone of the Free City economy.
- Prices differ a little from city to city. In Gangseo, which holds the wind farms, electricity is cheap; in Yeongdeungpo, which hosts the market, goods are cheap. Traders live on closing the gap between the two.

### 11.3 The Arc: daily charge allowances and maintenance queue places

- There is no market inside the Arc. Meals are shared out by the inner-circle houses and the maintenance units (01-core 5.3). Instead the Arc counts by what keeps the body's machines alive (provisional T04-52).
- A **daily charge allowance** is one baptised person's daily share of electricity, 8 W for 24 hours, about 0.2 kWh (01-core 4.2). When the Arc trades with outsiders, electricity is counted in daily charge allowances.
- A **maintenance queue place** is one maintenance session at a maintenance unit. One 30-day check is one turn. The Arc sells maintenance queue places to outsiders too. That machine wearers in the Government and the Free Cities must in the end reach the Arc (01-core 7.3) becomes a price here.

### 11.4 The design yardstick: person-days

To put the three counts in one table, this bible uses a design yardstick, the **person-day**. One person-day is what one person needs to live a day on the platform layer: a day's meals and 10 L of water. The person-day is not money in the world. People in the world speak of meals, kWh and daily charge allowances (provisional T04-53).

| Government 1 workday (ordinary) | 1 person-day | |
| Free City 1 kWh | about 0.67 person-day | By definition: at market, one person-day of meals and water is 1.5 kWh |
| Free City 1 labour hour (ordinary) | about 0.33 person-day | 0.5 kWh |
