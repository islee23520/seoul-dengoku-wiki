## Common Forms and Variants

In the field the Communication-Root Fungal Bodies showed as a gray-white film and slender bundles of teal fiber. Rather than boring through the optical fiber itself, it grew along the scratches of the sheath, the borders of the splice gel, and the condensation faces of the metal fixtures. Under work lamps its color barely changed, but when a steady pulse train flowed, a weak potential difference could be measured.

The Communication-Root Fungal Bodies' common ecology responded passively to facility surfaces and the local environment. The G21E identifiers marked biomechanical variants whose movement and work functions had differentiated within that environment; they were not treated as one individual with the common film or the whole installation.

### G21E01 · Pulse-Logic Weaving Worm

- **Role · body:** A six-segment weaving body moving along fiber-cable sheaths. Its foreleg ceramic combs bound severed hyphae, and the piezo pouch on its back sensed vibration and electrical noise as it repaired the swarm's communication routes.
- **Origin:** It followed the Seoul creative setting of the Communication-Root Fungal Bodies, born as damaged communication lines and root-fungus tissue tangled in the S05 Yeouido Line corridor after the collapse. The irregular signals left in the dead lines set the growth direction, but it understood no signal content and identified no sender.
- **Habitat:** The cable racks of the Gwangjin Line underground utility tunnel were the core habitat. In the monsoon it chased humidity into the Yeouido Line drainage corridor, and when strong pressure was sensed from the XT01 outskirts it withdrew into the inner conduit, weaving the passages dense.
- **Needs:** It fed on the faint heat beneath the sheath and on a little organic adhesive, spending leftover energy charging the piezo pouch. Its feeding ground easily overlapped the HP07 waste-cable recovery work, since growth stopped at the resource ceiling.
- **Life · maintenance:** It passed the five stages of emergence, spread, stagnation, negotiable, and dissolution. With metal dust caught in the ceramic comb it over-tightened knots and crushed sound circuits too, and washing the comb with coolant mist could steady its activity before dissolution.
- **Behavior · hierarchy:** One thickest weaver served as controller, conducting the swarm with knot vibration. When F05's synthesized sensor logs clashed with field traces, it waited for the HP07 human mediator's test signal, and it never saw through beyond vibration and electrical noise.
- **Escalation:** In stage one it hung loose fiber rings as warning; in stage two it caused communication delay and intermittent line breaks. When HP07 demolished its marked feeding ground without notice it rose to stage three, sealing the utility-tunnel mouth — the moment just before the negotiation window closed.
- **Engagement · counter:** A steady low-frequency noise lured the swarm toward empty cables, and after lowering piezo output with local cooling, routes could be sealed with insulating tape. Laying a detour feeding line and separating only the core knots did less communication harm than indiscriminate cutting.
- **Negotiation · moral choice:** HP07 could trade waste sheath and organic adhesive for two night hours of utility-tunnel passage. Removing it all raised immediate recovery volume but took with it the ecological mending that had bound the lines' fine cracks — and the citizens' contact net.
- **Scenario links:** In G21-SC1 it stood as both the key temporarily rejoining a severed rescue signal and the passage obstacle. Preserving the designated feeding line opened a non-lethal detour into the G21-SC3 sealed Gwangjin Line section.

## Habitat Record

In the field the Communication-Root Fungal Bodies showed as a gray-white film and slender bundles of teal fiber.

Rather than boring through the optical fiber itself, it grew along the scratches of the sheath, the borders of the splice gel, and the condensation faces of the metal fixtures.

Under work lamps its color barely changed, but when a steady pulse train flowed, a weak potential difference could be measured.

Its sensing range was sharply limited.

The colony responded only to the time interval between optical pulses and to changes in surrounding temperature and moisture activity.

It could not distinguish packets' contents or addresses, line owners, or communication purposes, and it understood neither names nor commands.

The stabilizing action began with ionic movement in the living tissue.

When repeated pulse intervals entered the permitted range, the film's internal charge distribution slowly aligned, easing the micro electrochemical swings and hot spots around the splice.

As a result, even a damaged splice could show steady signal-arrival intervals.

Because of that effect, an inspector had to measure actual cable state and the colony's laid-on stability separately.

Watching only normal traffic made compensated cracks hard to find.

One had to secure a short test section, shake the pulse interval in stages, heat or dehumidify only one side of the junction box, and record where the jitter revived.

Colonies grown in different places did not respond alike to the same conditions.

When a film seated in a low-temperature conduit and a film in a high-temperature relay room met at one patch panel, their passive reactions to hold different heat gradients overlapped.

Then, rather than the signal vanishing, intermittent crosstalk, irregularly ordered delay, and errors at particular intervals appeared.

Separation work needed physical design, not biological instruction.

Technicians inserted dry ceramic sleeves between splice trays and set thin heat baffles and independent dehumidification lines.

The control team reassigned lines so the interval bands each colony stabilized never overlapped on one dark route.

Operating agreements ran between people too.

Facility managers set the hours and temperature limits for opening junction boxes; communication operators presented the dark routes available for detour and the permitted delay.

Record keepers left measurements before and after tests, cable-cut positions, and data-transfer moments, assigning the colony no schedule or record-keeping role.

Contaminated cable raised its own difficulty.

Even where the colony worked stably on a salt- and metal-dust-fouled sheath, tensile-wire corrosion and splice-gel breakdown could continue beneath.

Removing the cable at once could cut signal continuity; leaving it grew the chance of sudden shorting.

Long-term, laying new cable in parallel, verifying section by section, and handing over the data flow was the most predictable course.

But independent measurement before and after the switch was needed, so the old colony's corrections were never mistaken for the new line's performance.

The final removal time was decided by human managers from corrosion rate, detour capacity, and record-continuity verification — not by the colony's state.
