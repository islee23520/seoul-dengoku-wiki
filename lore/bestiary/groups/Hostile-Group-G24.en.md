## Common Forms and Variants

The first signs usually appeared not as biological movement but as facility anomalies. A steady pulsation sounded in suction tubes that should have been empty, the membrane of a decommissioned perfusion cassette slowly swelled, and the water level of floor drain traps rose and fell in time with pump starts. Wet tissue fragments set no direction of their own, but they blocked valve gaps or bridged electrodes and changed the machines' judgment conditions.

The Medical-Tissue Machine Swarm's common ecology responded passively to facility surfaces and the local environment. The G24E identifiers marked biomechanical variants whose movement and work functions had differentiated within that environment; they were not treated as one individual with the common film or the whole installation.

## Habitat Record

The first signs usually appeared not as biological movement but as facility anomalies.

A steady pulsation sounded in suction tubes that should have been empty, the membrane of a decommissioned perfusion cassette slowly swelled, and the water level of floor drain traps rose and fell in time with pump starts.

Wet tissue fragments set no direction of their own, but they blocked valve gaps or bridged electrodes and changed the machines' judgment conditions.

What the collective could sense was pressure, flow, conductivity, temperature, turbidity, and power state alone.

It distinguished no faces, posts, or requests.

Cases that looked like reactions to alarm tones were in fact driven by voltage changes or pump-stop signals arising in the same circuits.

The core actions were the five routines of washing, pressurization, perfusion, drainage, and preservation.

No routine could make a new order of its own; it followed the paths the sensor thresholds and wiring allowed under the supervision keys.

So in the field its actions could never be fully predicted.

Rather than deciding, one had to physically trace which input ran to which relay and valve.

Fluid isolation was reviewed before power cutoff.

With power down, force remained in elastic membranes, gravity head, and accumulators, and auxiliary batteries could sense flow change late.

The supply and recovery sides had to be locked separately, then the middle section depressurized into recovery bags, before piping could be separated safely.

Aerosolization risk grew when blocked lines were opened hastily.

Vents, sinks, and overflow drain traps in particular became bypass outlets of automatic pressure correction.

Workers checked gauges behind transparent shields and opened only one low-pressure recovery path at a time.

Record recovery was a separate engineering problem.

Corridor-net controllers judged damaged blocks as cleaning targets, and access from ordinary terminals could send modification signals back with queries.

An electrically isolated optical receiver let the original device keep running while blocking the automatic cleanup commands.

Capture of essential devices often began at shared docking rails and wet contacts.

Controllers mistook connected devices not as equipment for people but as additional pumps or storage.

On separation, power lines and fluid couplings were checked before communication lines, and nothing returned to its original position until an independent battery start test finished.

Supervision keys were not symbols of authority but physical parts limiting circuits.

Washing, drainage, and movement keys were shaped not to be inserted simultaneously, and each stage's completion was confirmed on separate gauges.

Coordination among field leads focused on which function to allow first, and the collective neither agreed with nor demanded payment for those decisions.

On finding unidentified tissue fragments, preservation and evacuation could demand the same equipment.

Holding low-temperature perfusion cut the service carrier's power and loading space; cutting preservation input sent the fragments into the waste flow.
