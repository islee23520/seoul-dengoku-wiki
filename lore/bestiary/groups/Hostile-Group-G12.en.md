## Common Forms and Variants

The Microfiber Dermis Swarm was an industrial residual ecology moving between the Sinnae freight hall's brake dust and the Yeouido treatment-filter gallery. Its skin was a temporary filter layer holding static charge; cover, grounding, and washing teams divided the tension lines, and when two lines crossed the danger value anyone could stop the crane. In fully sealed clean sections the layers split and it could not stay long.

Ordinary rigging and water-supply workers were affected workers with fiber layers attached, not monsters occupying the entrance. The named individuals from G12E01 on were variants inheriting specialized organs — sensory plates, silk glands, comb bones, keratin record plates — grading surfaces, spinning cocoons, or pressing fiber bricks. The work knot's stop right and the field authority of surveyors and knot-mothers, though overlapping in view, never commanded the ordinary work teams.

### Role · body
A humanoid variant surveying the Jamsil Line's surface safety, pressing the thin skin plates of palms and soles onto surfaces to read friction, acidity, and the direction of fiber ridges. One contact read only a hand-span of trace, and it could not distinguish underwater states, airborne contamination, or footprints older than a day.

### Origin
As industrial microfiber repeatedly bored into skin wounds, the lesions of hands and feet changed into sensory organs making different membranes by direction of damage. Jamsil Line residents used this ability to find safe walls and floors, and a surveyor class formed.

### Habitat
It circuited the outer walls, storeroom floors, and cracked corridors of the Jamsil Line core habitat, and in migration season tested the surfaces of the Water Compact junction first. Soft mud mixed records easily, so it left thin film markers on stone or metal edges.

### Needs · wants
It needed fat to regenerate sensory plates, weakly acidic water for washing, and resin to cut friction, but hand and foot plates could be renewed only four a day. Unable to find safe surfaces long, it concentrated survey resources around its own nest and gave up outer corridors.

### Life cycle · upkeep
Adults stacked used sensory plates in resin ponds, and when ten gathered they raised a juvenile in between. The pond had to stay dry two months to raise one juvenile; short of resin, the skin layers stuck together and died.

### Behavior · rank
The scout that had read the most surface types graded the corridors, and subordinates marked results with film scraps of different colors. Scouts did not lead columns, and sanctioned those ignoring the markers by withholding later survey results.

### Staged threat
In stage one it took resin and washing water; in stage two it smeared irritant film on handles and floors to flay passers-by's skin; in stage three it flipped hazard markers to drive several groups onto weak floors or corrosive zones.

### Counter · combat
Laying thin plates of differing materials layered on the floor overloaded the limited contact reading and slowed the survey. Forcibly peeling the sensory plates left it unable to walk for long, so weakly acidic water was sprayed to loosen adhesion and the survey tools separated.

### Negotiation · moral choice
Given a resin tag with three grooves and rationed washing water, the scout left survey sections and validity times as film markers and provided results for one season to groups holding the same tag. Residents gained safe-floor information and the scout resources for its plates; but if resin supply stopped it turned that tag's markers to hazard marks, so one had to decide whether to slow another settlement's survey order to protect one corridor.

### Scenario links
In G12-SC1 the Jamsil Line's resource split and H12's surface-damage records decided the film markers' credibility, and HC12's status accord fixed the survey zone. Seasonal world-link roads ran to the Water Compact, and as the outer-pressure variable XT02 grew the Film-Scout declared only the core nest's surroundings safe.

### Role · body
A humanoid variant handling wound sealing and nursing of the Microfiber Dermis Swarm, drawing adhesive thread from split skin lesions and wrapping the injured like cocoons. Its finger projections adjusted the thread's thickness and tension, but its clouded eyes could not distinguish movement and color change beyond two steps, so it depended on smell for infection.

### Origin
As industrial microfiber accumulated in wounds, skin glands that had pushed out foreign matter reorganized into suture-thread organs. The reaction that had closed its own lesions widened into the habit of wrapping nearby individuals for preservation.

### Habitat
It hung recovery cocoons in the warm dry rooms of the Jamsil Line core habitat and used the space beneath the bridges on the Water Compact routes as temporary nurseries in seasonal change. With humidity high the thread loosened, so it abandoned at once any spot where three drops fell from the ceiling.

### Needs · wants
One bundle of thread took one meal of protein and a pinch of salts, and an adult produced only enough per day to wrap one person. Short of resources it first sutured patients with steady breathing and familiar body odor.

### Life cycle · upkeep
Among the skin scraps left inside a failed recovery cocoon, one with intact silk glands could grow into a juvenile. Adults opened their own lesions to draw thread, so after ten whole-body cocoons the damaged parts hardened and they could breed no more.

### Behavior · rank
The keeper with the most patients actually awake stood high in the nursing order, and subordinates procured feed and salts. It could mistake a weakly moving stranger for a patient, but touching a refusal band scented with vinegar three times spared that subject from wrapping.

### Staged threat
In stage one it collected bandages and food protein; in stage two it bound limbs with thread for forced rest; in stage three it joined the exits into a giant cocoon membrane, leaving the creatures inside in long-term preservation.

### Counter · combat
A weak alkaline mist lowered adhesion so cocoons could be unwound without cutting. Cutting all tension at once with a blade opened the patients' suture sites, so the knots had to be wetted from the outside in.

### Negotiation · moral choice
Handing a vinegar-scented knot band and a salt pouch and knocking twice on the cocoon to release, the keeper learned that order as a nursing rule and freed designated patients. The keeper gained treatment material and residents recovered the injured; but if the next ration failed it took band-holders as priority cocoon subjects, so one had to choose whether to bear even enemies' treatment or break the accord.

### Scenario links
In G12-SC2 the Jamsil Line nursery confirmed who lay in the cocoons through H12's intake records, and HC12's status adjustment set the salt ration and release order. When the Water Compact world-link road closed and XT02 rose, the Scar-Silkworm Keeper classified all passers-by as preservation resources.

### Role · body
A humanoid variant building the windbreak walls and catch fences of the Microfiber Dermis Swarm, raking scattered fiber with the comb bones of its arms and spraying neck-pouch mucus to harden it in layers. ### Origin
As industrial microfiber combined with skin cracks into hard reinforcement layers, the behavior of peeling them off and attaching them to outer surfaces arose. Jamsil Line residents got walls blocking dust and beasts and, in exchange, fed mucus and repair material to settle the habit.

### Habitat
It raised fiber walls between thresholds and low frames in the Jamsil Line core habitat, and in dry seasons made semicircular windbreaks outside the Water Compact's temporary camps. On high towers or wide flats it lacked anchor points and nested only around existing stakes.

### Needs · wants
One wall panel took two sacks of dry fiber, a pail of water for mucus, and fat to prevent skin cracks. An adult completed only one panel in three days, and short of water it tore the shared windbreak to reinforce its own egg wall.

### Life cycle · upkeep
A juvenile grew inside a sealed wall alternately coated with two adults' mucus layers, taking a season to complete. While making the egg wall the parents could not repair other structures, and if moisture seeped in, juvenile and stored fiber rotted together.

### Behavior · rank
The individual that had built the longest-enduring fence set the anchor-use order, and subordinates handled fiber collection and crack filling. Orders passed as stake-tapping sounds of set counts, but in downpours the signal drowned and each defended only the nearest wall.

### Staged threat
In stage one it dismantled cloth and rope for material; in stage two it bound ankles and weapons to the fence with mucus-fiber bands; in stage three it blocked both ends of a corridor and narrowed the inner wall, shutting intruders into dust and heat.

### Counter · combat
The mucus layer swelled with citrus solution, so pouring a little on the seams and pushing along the comb direction quietly separated the wall. Fire spread the dry fiber through the whole corridor, so the anchor stakes were pulled to lay the structure flat and it was covered with wet cloth.

### Negotiation · moral choice
Offering four stake taps together with a blue fiber bundle, the warden learned that anchor as a joint-repair zone and maintained the residents' windbreak while receiving rationed water. Both sides gained protection and stable material; but if the promised pails ran dry it turned the blue fiber into capture markers, so one had to decide whether to allow egg-wall repair or monopolize the settlement's defense resources.

### Scenario links
In G12-SC3 the Jamsil Line's anchor placement and H12's wind records exposed the fence's weak seams, and HC12's status accord set the joint-repair range. With XT02 pressure added to the Water Compact world-link road, the Fiber-Fence Warden sealed even refuge corridors as egg-wall material.

The broker holding rare, recent contamination traces set prices and traffic information, and debtors left their own skin plates as collateral. ### Staged threat
In stage one it bundled solvent and food as preconditions of other trades; in stage two it sent enemies into skin-lesion zones with outdated safety plates; in stage three it ground toxic keratin and made a contact-dermatitis layer on the market floor.

Washing the keratin plates from the edges in order with neutral solvent exposed the component layers by time and confirmed forgery. Holding out a solvent bottle with a triangular groove and a collateral plate engraved with a return date, the broker re-engraved the terms on the keratin edge and provided verified routes to buyers with the same groove. The broker gained nutrient salts and trade trust and the buyer contamination data; but past the date it published the collateral plate's scent and movement traces, so one had to decide whether to sacrifice a debtor's privacy to keep market order or erase the trade records.

### Scenario links

## Habitat Record

Beneath the Neutral Convoy City's east freight elevator ran a corridor where the work shift table was more accurate than the map.

When freight from the Mangu-side lines passed the Sinnae depot perimeter's hoist shafts, brake dust, packaging fiber, and powdered rope wore a gray film on the low ceilings.

On days the dust collector ran right, the film only thinned and trembled, but when its intake sound fell, people looked for covers before the warning lamps.

The industrial residual ecology living between this section and the Yeouido south-end treatment-filter gallery was the G12 Microfiber Dermis Swarm.

Their skin was no shell accumulating dust without end.

It was closer to a temporary filter layer — weaving a fine static-charged stratum, washing it, drying it, and weaving it again.

Thickness and color spoke only of exposure conditions, proving neither character nor status.

The dermis swarm was first confirmed after the Sinnae freight hall's rigging units and the Water Compact's filter-replacement teams began using the same recovery cloth.

Fiber on workers' and carriers' skin loosened in water and, in dry corridors, formed mesh structure again.

Early on this was recorded as a contamination riot, but as washing slots became trade and recovered layers became auxiliary filter media, a separate ecology ledger arose.

The dermis swarm did not occupy one place and widen.

When the Sinnae hall dried, the static team twisted wall fiber like ground wire; when the Yeouido backwash schedule opened, the washing team untied the knots and moved them to the Han-side underground filter gallery.

In fully sealed clean sections the layers split, the senses dulled, and it could not stay long.

In seasons of heavy dust inflow from the West Sea Granary docks, it cut the Sinnae-side protected area first under outer pressure.

Its senses began in the skin, not the eyes.

As airborne particles grew, the view clouded milky; as charge leaked from cables, the arm and neck fiber layers stood first.

It read the fine tremble of rigging cables, the heat of dust-collector motors, and the smell of carbonized fiber, but it did not know states beyond walls.

The dust graphs that synthetic H12 Ichaeon produced were important reference values, not verdicts.

Optical sensors could mistake moisture for dust, or the dermis swarm could miss signals from over-drying.

When Ichaeon's differential-pressure logs clashed with the rigging supervisor's tension records, the values were never averaged into compromise.

The freight was lowered to the floor, the cables made load-free, and both measured again.

The survival economy's ceiling was clear.

Without washing water, replacement filter media, small organic oil, and grounding scrap, the dermis swarm could move no farther.

Washing water was allocated matching the Yeouido water-supply ledger's tonnage and never mixed with crane coolant hoses.

Static scrap too was counted not by weight but by the length of grounding rods actually buried.

Dust-collector power was permitted only to the intake stages needed, and added dust load was not solved by seizing surrounding corridors.

When media ran out, instead of covering new zones it thinly peeled old skin layers and turned them into common filter.

This choice was a debt trading body protection for logistics.

The Geumcheon District Office Machinery House's large-rigging qualification often clashed with HC12 over this process, but the rigging ledger and the dust ledger could not be merged.

The right to run a crane did not carry the right to divert washing water.

The decision right lay not in a rank table but in the work knot.

The cover team took the protective plane between freight and people, the grounding team the charge path from cable to floor, the washing team the recovery route of loosened fiber and water.

The three teams shared different tension lines through the knots of wrists and waists, and when two or more lines crossed the danger value anyone could declare a crane stop.

Even whoever read the collection rhythm best only proposed a target concentration and could not open a corridor alone.

A worker with a split skin layer lost no authority.

Knowing which part tore first with the body, they rather set that cover's disassembly order.

But at night, without a supervisor and a water-supply record keeper together, no new work knot could be made.

This limit kept the swarm from endlessly growing under the pretext of emergency.

The crisis began not when fiber clung to walls but when water and freight demanded the same exit.

The gray film on walls was a cleaning signal, but a knot in a crane cable meant lowering speed and re-grounding.

When backwash water swept the Sinnae dust corridor, loosened fiber pushed into the Yeouido treatment filters and raised differential pressure.

Yet cutting backwash dried skin layers and cables together, vulnerable to the smallest spark.

At maximum pressure the dermis swarm did not seal the entrance.

It set every load on the floor and stopped transfer itself.

Its way was to force the choice — disclose the possibility of water contamination, or bear the cost of stopping logistics.

G12-SC1 'The Stopped Cranes' dealt with the moment three cranes of the Sinnae freight hall stopped at once.

It opened as gray fiber knots hung on each cable.

Field staff could force one crane through to keep the medical-equipment shipping contract, lower all and re-plant the grounding rods, or cut only the knots to keep the load.

Forcing tore the cover and left the next shift's protected skin area permanently short; a full stop delayed the medical equipment being restarted by a day.

Cutting looked fastest but left airborne fiber and residual charge on the hall floor, raising insurance cost and grounding time for all later work.

The cost that never vanished was not the delay itself but whose safety had first been pushed outside the contract.

In G12-SC2 'The Backwash Backflow,' backwash water from Yeouido flowed backward into the freight hall and pushed loosened fiber toward the water-supply filters.

If field staff dumped the water, the fiber stayed in Sinnae but that day's drinking-water production fell.

Returning the water to the treatment gallery and disclosing the differential-pressure rise at once told citizens of the filter risk and shook water-supply trust.

Separating the washing hose from the water main and guiding recovery cloths manually was possible, but rigging stopped dead for a day.

No choice could trade water for media and make them never have been.

At least one of the dumped backwash tonnage, the disclosed pressure records, or the unfulfilled freight contracts remained in next season's ledger.

G12-SC3 'The Grounding Knot' occurred when two cables' tension crossed the danger value and a worker with a split skin layer unwound part of their own protective layer into a grounding knot and declared a full rigging stop.

If field staff recognized it as a just stop right, new protective-equipment standards and compensation responsibility arose, but the next shift's cooperation held.

Treating it as unauthorized work cut the immediate contract responsibility, but the dermis swarm untied the grounding knot and withdrew, and whoever rushed the restart bore the later static accidents.

Passing the load to the West Sea dock dust cover via XT02 lowered the Sinnae and Yeouido pressure but fixed a season's contamination burden on the outer labor zones.

The unrecovered water and the skin layers needing time to re-weave could not be returned at once by after-the-fact compensation.

Coexistence lay not in cleanly erasing the dermis swarm but in not hiding the price of stopping.

Cutting the cover with blades scattered fiber into the air; pushing it with high-pressure water only moved contamination to the filters.

The safe response ran in order — lower the load first, extend the grounding rods, then run low-pressure washing water into floor recovery cloths.

Dust-collector intake too was raised in stages, and loosened fiber was sealed as separate media, not freight packaging.

A worker's skin traces remained not as markers deciding passage order or trust but only as data computing needed cover area and equipment grade.

The Geumcheon District Office Machinery House, the Sinnae transfer record keeper, and the Yeouido water-supply manager had to disclose lost time, dumped water, and unreusable protected area in the same meeting.

The moment those numbers were hidden, it was not only the dermis swarm that broke, but the continuity of rigging and drinking water joining the two cities.
