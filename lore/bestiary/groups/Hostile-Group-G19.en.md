## Common Forms and Variants

The Coolant-Index Fungal Body held most stably on metal surfaces where flow was gentle — heat-exchanger fronts, valve pockets, the protective sleeves of gauges. Its thickness usually stayed thin, but where stagnation ran long, carbonates and corrosion products piled into a mucoid matrix and made a hard layer. The surface's color was no biological signal but the result of the bound metal and its oxidation state.

The common ecology of the Coolant-Index Fungal Body responded passively to facility surfaces and the local environment. The G19E identifiers marked biomechanical variants whose movement and work functions had differentiated within that environment; they were not treated as one individual with the common film or the whole installation.

- **Role · body:** A biomechanical cleaning species crawling over cooling-pipe surfaces, marking corrosion positions in linear patterns. The siliceous brush under its flat body scraped biofilm for food, and the thin metal flakes on its back folded with temperature and pressure changes, leaving a travel record.
- **Origin:** It arose after the collapse in the coolant corridor joining the Yongsan and Noryangjin lines, where microbial film, rusted inspection tags, and the wreckage of automatic tube-cleaning devices combined. An ecological product peculiar to Seoul with no bearing on past facilities' accountability, it took the remaining piping as its habitat base.
- **Habitat:** The heat-exchanger detour pipes and the inside of inspection ports on the Yongsan Line were its core range. In summer it moved into the Noryangjin Line's underground cooling pipes as pressure fell, and when pressure waves came in from the XT04 outskirts it withdrew behind the nearest closed valve.
- **Needs:** It could act only taking in lukewarm coolant, iron ions, and thin organic film together. It sometimes occupied first the waste pipes and coolant HP05 recovered, but laying rusted iron mesh in a feed detour separated it from the work zone.
- **Life · maintenance:** It passed through the five stages of emergence, spread, stagnation, negotiable, and dissolution, and its numbers never exceeded the iron and biofilm total inside the pipes. When the record flakes on its back clung thick, it mislabeled sound pipe as corroded, so it had to be soaked in low-temperature water and peeled with a non-metallic brush.
- **Behavior · hierarchy:** One controller, called the plate-core, set the scraping rhythm, and several work swarms took different pipes. It sensed vibration and heat but could not know directly beyond a wall; where its patterns disagreed with F03's corrosion verdicts, the on-site confirmation of an HP05 human mediator took precedence.
- **Escalation:** In stage one it warned by leaving linear scrapes around inspection ports; in stage two it shook the circulation pumps' flow and disturbed infrastructure. Erasing its markings or abruptly moving the plate-core raised it to stage three, sealing the valve room — the last moment before the negotiation window closed.
- **Engagement · counter:** Effective practice guided the swarm into an empty detour with steady low-frequency noise, cooled it, and sealed the inspection port with a resin band. Rather than cutting the main pipe, changing its route with a feed tray and a blocking plate avoided losses to the citizens' cooling network.
- **Negotiation · moral choice:** HP05 could offer a bundle of waste iron mesh and eighteen minutes of no-passage time in exchange for the true leak map the swarm had marked. Indiscriminate removal took the corrosion-watch and biofilm-decomposition functions with it, and the Yongsan Line's cooling efficiency worsened over the long run.
- **Scenario links:** In G19-SC1 it appeared as the on-site clue distinguishing false corrosion marks. In G19-SC2 it was the counterpart in negotiating HP05 recovery squads' passage times, and in G19-SC3 it played the living alarm moving ahead of the XT04 pressure wave to signal outer anomalies.

## Habitat Record

The Coolant-Index Fungal Body held most stably on metal surfaces where flow was gentle — heat-exchanger fronts, valve pockets, the protective sleeves of gauges.

Its thickness usually stayed thin, but where stagnation ran long, carbonates and corrosion products piled into a mucoid matrix and made a hard layer.

The surface's color was no biological signal but the result of the bound metal and its oxidation state.

The chemical memory this fungal body left was no continuous record.

Under high-flow washing or oxidant dosing some layers stripped away, and the sediment formed after covered the vacancy.

F03 had to use cross-section analysis, pipe position, and flow direction together; color alone could not fix an event's hour.

Its sensing range was limited to the contact surface.

The body's viscosity and sediment bonding shifted with temperature, acidity, dissolved oxygen, metal ions, chlorine oxidants, and shear.

It could not know a distant branch's state or predict the next operating cycle, and it classified no meaning.

In normal operation the subtlest problem was sensor fouling.

When a thin film inside a protective sleeve changed acidity and redox potential more slowly than the surrounding coolant, the sensor put out steady numbers.

Meanwhile in a narrow far branch corrosion products could grow and flow fall, splitting the on-screen normal values from the true thermal state.

As film thickened in a low-flow branch the pressure loss grew and the flow fell further.

Flakes shed in the process caught on downstream orifices and strainers, changing even other branches' flows.

In high heat load, a small distribution error reached outlet temperature rise, and neighboring branches could lose cooling in sequence.

In the field a sacrificial bypass meant to take the fouling was designed broader and lower than the main circuit.

When the valve crew steered the shock water there by pressure difference, the fungal layers and the rough metal surface caught metal ions, mineral grains, and flakes.

This was not the body accepting terms but the result of people adjusting the fluid path and contact area.

Bypass operation had clear limits.

The more it trapped, the narrower the pipe's effective cross-section; isolated too late, the piled matter dropped back into the main circuit at once.

The crew timed replacement on F03's pressure-difference estimates and sample analysis, but the actual valve order and cooling-capacity split followed the field heat load first.

When a heat-exchanger wall cracked, the problem changed character.

With pressure higher on the coolant side, chemicals and fungal fragments moved into the drinking water and could consume residual disinfectant.

With pressure higher on the drinking side, the cooling circuit diluted and the corrosion-inhibition balance broke — either way demanded immediate pressure separation and point-of-use shutoff.

After an accident, whether to preserve the index spool or return it to cooling service became the weighty choice.

A preserved pipe gave data narrowing the order of water-quality shocks and pressure reversals, but the cooling area usable while sealed shrank.

Emergency opening restored heat removal but washed the layered traces away and could shake the contamination boundary again, so the crew first computed lives, drinking-water safety, and the immediately needed thermal capacity, then weighed them against F03's diagnostic value.
