## Common Forms and Variants

In facilities the Dead-Battery Metal Colony settled, the zones where metal dissolved away and the zones where metal returned as crystal fell clearly apart. Around spent-battery pouches and solder dross the surface pitted gray-white, while on ceramic edges a few meters away copper resin and nickel shells layered thick. This separation was made by invisible potential difference and capillary moisture.

The Dead-Battery Metal Colony's common ecology responded passively to facility surfaces and the local environment. The G22E identifiers marked biomechanical variants whose movement and work functions had differentiated within that environment; they were not treated as one individual with the common film or the whole installation.

- **Role · body:** A biomechanical collector precipitating metal salts flowed out of dead batteries onto the porous comb plate of its back, making them the colony's structural stock. Its pincers read heat and the vibration needles at its mouth read floor resonance, but it could not learn of resources behind insulating walls.
- **Origin:** After the collapse, dead batteries, leaked electrolyte, and microbial film piled in the S06·Gangnam Line corridor combined into the Dead-Battery Metal Colony. A Seoul creative origin reflecting modern anxieties over spent-battery metal precipitation colonies, not a setting blaming any real corporation or current regime.
- **Habitat:** It used the floor cracks and drainage grooves of the central Seoul Station spent-battery sorting yard as its core habitat. In the rainy season it moved seasonally to the Gangnam Line's lower warehouses where metal salts flowed, and when strong heat and vibration pressure came from the XT02 outskirts it moved its comb plate inside the yard.
- **Needs:** It needed dead batteries' residual energy, coolant, and scrap iron for the comb plate's skeleton. It clashed directly with HP08's safe recovery work, but supplied with fully discharged batteries and contaminated coolant separated, it limited its activity range to the resource ceiling.
- **Life · maintenance:** It changed through emergence–spread–stagnation–negotiable–dissolution. When the comb plate's drain holes clogged, metal salts lumped to one side as a 'polarization horn'; low-temperature washing and neutralized coolant let it strip its dangerous precipitates off itself.
- **Behavior · hierarchy:** One controller individual adjusted the comb plate's potential difference, and the collecting swarms carried metal salts only from the designated grooves. Even if F06 alarmed at every precipitation reaction as runaway, once the HP08 mediator measured temperature and leakage on site, that judgment took precedence.
- **Escalation:** In stage one it left white metal-salt lines in the drainage grooves as warning. When HP08 lifted batteries without the agreed discharge procedure, stage two had metal crystals clogging sensors and drains, and breaking the controller's comb plate raised stage three, sealing the sorting yard's entrance with a thick precipitation wall.
- **Engagement · counter:** Cooling spray lowered precipitation speed, then fully discharged batteries were set as bait in the safe zone. Vibration noise made the pincers flinch and the drainage grooves were sealed by section; dumping large volumes of water could spread metal salts into the citizens' drainage net.
- **Negotiation · moral choice:** HP08 could be granted contaminated-battery handling rights and passage times in exchange for assigning coolant and safe precipitation plates. Indiscriminately destroying the colony emptied the yard fast, but also removed the function that fixed leaked metal salts and slowed contamination's spread.
- **Scenario links:** In G22-SC1 it was the guide marking leaked batteries' positions with precipitation lines. In G22-SC2 it adjusted HP08's recovery volume and the colony's discharged-battery share, and in G22-SC3 it cooled and separated the entrance precipitation wall forming under XT02 pressure to keep the negotiation window open.

## Habitat Record

In facilities the Dead-Battery Metal Colony settled, the zones where metal dissolved away and the zones where metal returned as crystal fell clearly apart.

Around spent-battery pouches and solder dross the surface pitted gray-white, while on ceramic edges a few meters away copper resin and nickel shells layered thick.

This separation was made by invisible potential difference and capillary moisture.

The colony's base film was thin like damp dust.

The micro-pores inside held electrolyte, and the insulating organic film slowed water draining away all at once.

Metal ions moved along this film, but the colony as a whole set no direction.

Only the crystal growth rate differed by the potential, humidity, ion concentration, and temperature measured at each point.

Recovery facilities usually consisted of porous alumina bricks, exchangeable graphite plates, and tin-plated collection mesh.

Setting the bricks' pore sizes differently by zone made a moisture gradient, and holding the mesh's potential slightly lower than the surrounding structure made copper and nickel precipitate there first.

Raising voltage gave fast yield but also steeply raised the chance of dendrites crossing insulation gaps.

So skilled teams adjusted humidity before voltage.

Lowering the condensation plates' temperature and changing the ceramic wicks' length folded the ion paths slowly into the recovery tank.

In work that used no building distribution net, movement was induced only by separated sacrificial metal plates and concentration difference, and the residual charge of capacitors and uninterruptible power supplies was penned in separate discharge boxes.

An arc chain could start from a single fine dendrite.

When the first short evaporated the crystal, metal vapor and carbonized traces became the path of the next discharge, and adjacent terminals connected in turn.

Even with breakers working, charge left in the grounding net or shielding layers could carry short chains on, so stopping was never guaranteed without physical sectioning.

Structural corrosion was far quieter.

When copper collection mesh and iron anchors touched the same wet film, iron dissolved first and copper thickened.

Seen from the surface recovery looked good, but the cross-sections of rack fasteners and cable trays shrank from inside.

Load tests had to run separately from metal recovery volume, and the rebar potential under the ceramic recovery tanks kept being measured.

In record-keeping zones the electrical reference point itself was the object of preservation.

As metal piled on the shielding mesh the connectors' reference potential shifted, and readers could not tell normal signals from corrosion noise.

Even with files remaining, once the links of index keys, identity correspondence tables, and access histories broke, whose record it was could not be confirmed, so physical media and the attribution structure had to be protected together.

The field manager limited the colony's passive reactions with human measures — replacing sacrificial electrodes, installing ceramic drainage insulating partitions.

When the recovery tank saturated, the collection mesh was mechanically stripped off, and before new surfaces went in, the potential of the surrounding grounding and structure was matched again.

Late action had the clogged drains pushing moisture into the walls, making new galvanic pairs outside the recovery zone.

The final judgment usually took the form of abandoning one of two infrastructures first.

Permanently discharging the power modules kept the record environment but lost backup power; cutting the archive's pipes and shielding saved the power equipment but broke the records' long-term preservation conditions.

Either way, the cut faces had to be sealed with ceramic and non-conductive resin, removing detours for potential and moisture, before the next precipitation path could be controlled.
