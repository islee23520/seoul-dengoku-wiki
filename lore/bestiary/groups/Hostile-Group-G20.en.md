## Common Forms and Variants

The Iron-Scale Colony was no stain spread over steel surfaces but a thin laminate moving inside joints. Each lamella looked like a black-gray metal foil, its edges set with fine magnetic studs that interlocked with other pieces. It held most stably in the narrow spaces where compression and shear acted together — around bolt holes, between the toe laps of fillet welds.

The Iron-Scale Colony's common ecology responded passively to facility surfaces and the local environment. The G20E identifiers marked biomechanical variants whose movement and work functions had differentiated within that environment; they were not treated as one individual with the common film or the whole installation.

### G20E01 · Rail-Keratin Splicer

- Source batch: M031
- Legacy role group: Controller
- Links: {"states":["S04","S09"],"houses":["HP06"],"theaters":["XT05"]}

| Codex item | Record |

| Common/variant and command scope | The Rail-Keratin Splicer was a mobile biomechanical variant differentiated from the G20 common ecology. The command · coordination role applied only at the independent-unit scope, and stood in for neither the whole colony's ecological order nor human command. |

- **Role · body:** A biomechanical colony spanning rusty rails and steel frames with scale-form metal pieces to make temporary bridges. Six gripper legs stacked the plates, and the induction coil under its belly gave fine vibration to interlock the scale edges.
- **Origin:** After the collapse, carry rails, rust powder, and biological adhesive film left in the S04 Mapo coalition corridor combined into being. The habit of linking severed movement routes hardened into a survival behavior; no past organization's command chain was inherited.
- **Habitat:** The Ttukseom coalition's waste-freight platform and cracked rail junctions were the core habitat. In the monsoon it moved under the Mapo coalition's elevated structure, and when ground vibration grew on the XT05 outskirts it extended scale bridges into the low steel gaps.
- **Needs:** It used thin scrap iron, rust powder, and repeated floor vibration as resources. It clashed with the HP06 recovery squad over sheet iron, but laying out bent wire mesh and low-value bolts as designated stock divided the work lines.
- **Life · maintenance:** It passed the five stages of emergence, spread, stagnation, negotiable, and dissolution, and the count of new splicers never exceeded the surrounding scrap iron. When the induction coil's polarity twisted, the scale bridge reared up and hurt passers-by, so the electrical noise had to be cut and direction regained with cold steel plates.
- **Behavior · hierarchy:** One shift core served as controller and designated the gaps to join, and the swarms divided into a carrying team and a pressing team. It sensed vibration and electrical noise, and when the colony refused a rail F04 had marked safe, the human mediator ran a load test.
- **Escalation:** In stage one it raised scales on the rails as warning. Moving the stockpiled scrap without notice locked the switches in stage two, and pressing a bridge under construction with heavy equipment raised stage three, sealing the platform entrance.
- **Engagement · counter:** Lure the carrying team onto empty track with a steady floor beat and cut the return route with cooling plates and blocking timber. Offering an alternative scrap heap as a feeding detour, rather than burning the adhesive film, reduced damage to the infrastructure.
- **Negotiation · moral choice:** If HP06 yielded some cheap scrap and the dawn passage right, the colony reinforced the recovery route by day. Dismantling every bridge increased the scrap but also erased the temporary walkway the Ttukseom coalition's residents used, lengthening rescue and ration times.
- **Scenario links:** In G20-SC1 it appeared as the option of joining a severed platform to open a rescue route. In G20-SC2 it became the counterparty of HP06's scrap allocation bargaining, and in G20-SC3 it changed its reinforcement direction ahead of XT05 vibration, announcing the expected collapse point.

## Habitat Record

The Iron-Scale Colony was no stain spread over steel surfaces but a thin laminate moving inside joints.

Each lamella looked like a black-gray metal foil, its edges set with fine magnetic studs that interlocked with other pieces.

It held most stably in the narrow spaces where compression and shear acted together — around bolt holes, between the toe laps of fillet welds.

Under repeated load the lamellae lay down in the shear direction and stacked upon one another.

The lamination filled gaps of tens of micrometers and raised the contact surface's friction and stiffness.

As a result a joint's deformation might fall, but the force once passing through it was pushed to the neighboring bolt rows or the ends of the reinforcing plates.

An inspector reading only the reduced sag and judging the state improved could easily miss a fatigue crack already growing.

The fatigue memory the colony left was a material state with direction.

Along long-repeated load axes the lamellar overlaps and magnetic-domain micro-cracks aligned, and the array remained after the load stopped.

Fixing a specimen in resin and cutting its section let one infer past shear direction and the order of load transfer.

Conversely, mixing it away with a wire brush or grinder made this record unrecoverable.

Vibration promoted movement but did not produce the same response at every frequency.

Lamellar movement stood out mostly in solid-borne vibration of roughly 8–420 hertz, accumulating at pressure nodes and dense gaps rather than on large-amplitude free faces.

Stopping the pumps or cranes might look safe, yet as the natural frequencies changed, the lamellar positions the colony maintained could shift too.

That was why the modal shapes before and after a shutdown had to be compared.

Galvanic potential influenced the lamellae's alignment and bond.

Where galvanized members, carbon steel, and copper grounding tabs joined, conductive fibers lay along the potential gradient.

Grounding, beyond a mere electrical safety facility, became a condition of the colony's distribution, so separating the braid without notice could relax a wide range of lamination at once.

Then even the structural sensors' reference potential shook, and real deformation overlapped measurement error.

Field management began with visualizing the load path.

Strain gauges were not stuck to one member alone but arrayed across the neighboring bolt rows and bracing, and acoustic-emission sensors cross-checked new cracks' positions.

With hydraulic jacks the target load was split into stages, waiting at each until deflection and bolt axial force steadied.

Making a fully unloaded state in haste pulled the lamellar wedges out and caused shock transfer.

The sacrificial steel coupon was a replaceable receptor for drawing the colony out of the parent structure.

Thin low-carbon plates were machined with micro slots of differing width, and potential was controlled with insulating bushings and a limited grounding braid.

When enough lamellae had gathered, both sides were first pressed with rigid clamps to fix the lamination, then the braid cut and the plate separated.

Reverse the order and the captured lamellae scattered back into the joint.

Magnetic and vibratory capture was used around closed sections and cable ducts hard to approach.

A magnetic yoke laid a gentle gradient toward the steel cassette, and a piezo shaker applied narrow-band vibration not overlapping the structure's natural frequencies.

Stronger excitation raised no efficiency — the lamellae could shatter into brittle fragments.

The cassette mass and nearby bearing vibration were monitored together and a ceiling set.

In demolition judgment, immediate stability collided with preserving deformation history.

A bay at risk of collapse was separated with remote jacks and cutting gear, but the lamellar array and residual deformation beyond the cut line were first photographed, measured, and fixable as specimens.

Full demolition quickly lowered approach risk but erased the cause of load redistribution, leaving the same weakness in the redesign.

Conversely, keeping unstable members long for the record could let grounding loss or a small vibration change cause sudden load transfer, so the scope of preservation was limited to structurally isolatable sections.
